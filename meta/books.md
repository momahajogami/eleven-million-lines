Here is the thing about reading that nobody says plainly enough: you cannot grant it passively.

You can put a book in front of someone and it will do nothing. You can hand a child a tablet loaded with educational software and it will do approximately nothing. The access that reading gives you — the real access, the kind that changes what you can think and who you can talk to and what rooms you can walk into — that access requires a human being, patient and present, starting early, returning often, over years. It is expensive. It is the most expensive thing a society does, when it does it, and the most costly omission when it doesn't.

This is why reading is not a private matter. It never has been. Early childhood literacy is a question of government funding, of who teaches and who doesn't, of what a school building looks like and where it is located. It is a question of religion — who is permitted to read the holy text and who receives it only as spoken word, mediated by someone else. It is a question of civics in the most basic sense: a citizen who cannot read is not fully a citizen. Not because of any failing in them, but because the infrastructure of participation — the ballot, the contract, the newspaper, the manual — was built for readers.

Every expansion of reading access in history has been a political event. Compulsory education. The public library. The paperback revolution that put books in train stations and pharmacies. Each one was fought over, because someone with power understood that the existing distribution of readers served them.

Coding is the same fight, one layer up, happening now.

We have managed to put a device in nearly every hand. This is genuinely remarkable. But a device is not access, any more than a book sitting on a shelf is literacy. What we have largely failed to do is put a patient human teacher alongside the device, starting early, in the years when the brain is most open to new symbol systems. Instead we have given children games, which are beautifully designed read-only experiences — which give the feeling of agency within a closed system while leaving the system closed. This is the digital equivalent of reading aloud to someone for their whole life and never teaching them to sound out words.

The final level of any video game is coding. But you do not arrive there by playing. You arrive there by being taught.

This course is, among other things, an argument that the real thing is available. Not a toy version. Not a block-based simulator of coding. The actual source code of Unix, Quake, Blender — the materials that professional engineers read — opened up and walked through together, with context and history and a guide who has been inside them. This is what a literature course does with Chaucer. You do not have to be Geoffrey Chaucer to read him. You need someone to sit with you the first time and show you that the language, strange as it looks, is navigable.

The books unit exists because books are the model. The codex — pages bound at the spine, held in two hands, read front to back or in any order, annotated in the margins — is one of the most successful information technologies ever built. A book printed in 1450 is still readable. The floppy disk from 1990 is not. Paper has a lower error rate over centuries than any digital medium we have yet produced. The card catalogue, the footnote, the index, the bibliography — these are interfaces refined over five hundred years of use. BibTeX and Obsidian are their computational descendants, not their replacements.

Good technologies should be allowed to live. Books first among them. Not because the past was better, but because something that has worked for five hundred years has earned the right to be understood before it is discarded.

The fountain pen. The bicycle. The real instrument played by a human hand. The bifocals that let an old person read one more decade. These are not antiques. They are solutions that have not been superseded, whatever the market would prefer you believe.

Bibliontology, as a civic science, asks: what do we owe each other as readers? What does a society that takes reading seriously look like — not just in its schools but in its streets, its buildings, its libraries, its laws? And what does it look like when it stops taking reading seriously, when it hands out devices and calls it access, when it gamifies literacy and mistakes engagement for education?

This unit does not answer those questions. It holds them open, which is what books have always done best.
