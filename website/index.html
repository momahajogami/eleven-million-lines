Eleven Million Lines You Should Know

Code is writing.
Writing is how we talk to each other.
Writing is how we talk to the future.

A

A is for all of us.
Anyone can learn to read.

Reading is a skill. It takes practice. It took the whole world thousands of years to figure out writing — and now you get to learn it. That's not nothing.

Code

Code is writing.
People wrote it.
You can read it too.

Some of the most important things ever written are in code. Not because they are secret — because they were made that way. With care. With intention. Like a poem, or a letter, or a very good book.

Old

Some of this code is very old.
Older than your parents.
Maybe older than your grandparents.

It still runs. That means someone wrote something true — something that kept working even as the world changed around it. We call those things classics.

Read

We are going to read it.
Slowly.
Together.

Not to memorize it. Not to copy it. To understand it — the way you understand a person when you read their letters. To know what they were thinking, and why, and what they were trying to build.

You

You can do this.
You have always been able to do this.

Reading is not a talent. It is a practice. Every person who reads well was, once, someone who could not read at all.

For the grown-ups

Eleven Million Lines You Should Know is a university course about reading landmark codebases the way a literature student reads canonical texts — slowly, attentively, with historical and cultural context.

Eleven codebases. Eleven weeks. Early Unix. C compilers. GNU. Blender. LaTeX. Haskell. Assembly. The first programs Ada Lovelace ever wrote. The mathematics of Grothendieck and Lawvere made executable.

The course is paired with primary sources: Lions' Commentary, the GNU Manifesto, Turing's 1936 paper, Lovelace's 1843 notes. Code is not separate from culture. It never was.

Two tracks: one classical (terminal, vim, compile it yourself), one accompanied (any environment, more scaffolding). Both reach the same place.

  • First venue: Ohio
  • All materials open source
  • Designed for undergraduates, graduate students, and working programmers who want to go back to the source
