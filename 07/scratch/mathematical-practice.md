# On Becoming a Mathematician

*A practical document for students approaching Unit 07.*

---

This unit asks you to sit with mathematics that most professional mathematicians find difficult. Before you open Hatcher or read Grothendieck's *Esquisse*, it is worth saying something honest about how mathematics is actually done — not how it appears in finished proofs, but how it feels in the room where the work happens.

---

## The invincibility question

Students sometimes ask how to become an invincible mathematician, someone who proves theorems constantly and never gets stuck. The answer is that no such person exists. What exists instead is a practice — a set of habits cultivated over years that make the confusion productive rather than paralyzing.

Here is what that practice looks like from the inside.

---

## Read proofs until you can hear them

Not skim — read slowly, line by line, asking *why is this step allowed?* at every move.

The goal is not to collect theorems but to internalize the grammar of mathematical argument until it feels like a native language. A proof, read carefully, teaches you what counts as a reason. It teaches you the difference between "this seems right" and "this follows necessarily." That distinction is the whole subject.

Start with Euclid. The *Elements* is not an antique curiosity — it is the template. Every mathematical argument written since inherits its structure. Then number theory. Then whatever draws you. But read slowly. A page of real mathematics, read with attention, is a day's work.

---

## Write everything down by hand

This is not romantic advice. It is mechanical.

The hand is slower than the eye, which forces you to actually process each symbol rather than pattern-match past it. When you copy a proof by hand, step by step, you discover exactly where you do not understand it — because your hand stops. The eye would have slid past. The hand does not lie.

Grothendieck wrote obsessively. Ramanujan filled notebooks with results he had not yet proved, could not yet prove, was feeling his way toward. The notebook is not a record of the work. The notebook *is* the work.

---

## Get comfortable being stuck

Invincibility in mathematics is not the absence of confusion. It is the ability to sit inside confusion without panicking.

Terence Tao has written that the experience of doing real mathematics is mostly feeling lost, punctuated by brief moments of clarity. The skill is tolerating the lost feeling long enough for something to move. Most people leave before the movement happens. They conclude they are not math people. They were almost there.

Grothendieck described understanding as something that happens to you when you stop forcing it. He was not being mystical — or not only mystical. He was describing a genuine cognitive phenomenon: the problem that resists direct assault sometimes yields when you approach it from a different level of generality, or set it down and return to it with fresh eyes. The ability to wait is a mathematical skill.

---

## Pick one area and go deep before going wide

Number theory, topology, combinatorics — pick one and read a real textbook cover to cover, doing every problem. Not a survey. A deep cut.

Breadth comes naturally once you have depth somewhere. The student who understands one thing well has a reference point — a place from which the new territory can be measured. The student who has surveyed everything and understood nothing deeply is always lost. Depth first. Width follows.

For this unit, the relevant depth is *topology* — specifically algebraic topology as Hatcher presents it. You do not need to finish the book. You need to start at page one and not skip the problems.

---

## Teach what you learn, immediately

Explaining forces precision in a way that private study does not.

When you understand something privately, you can leave the gaps vague. When you explain it to someone else, the gaps become visible — to them, and then, with slight embarrassment, to you. The explanation is not downstream of the understanding. It is part of how the understanding becomes complete.

This is why the course asks you to write commentary, not just read. Writing a commentary on a mathematical concept is an act of mathematical practice, not a report on one.

---

## A note on this unit specifically

The mathematics here — Grothendieck's toposes, Lawvere's categories, the machinery of algebraic topology — was not designed to be easy. It was designed to be *right*, which is a different thing. The difficulty is not a barrier placed in front of the ideas; it is the shape of the ideas themselves.

You are not expected to master this. You are expected to make contact with it — to read slowly enough and carefully enough that you feel, at least once, what it is like to think at this level. That experience, even briefly, changes what you think mathematics is capable of.

That change is the point of the unit.
