We started with Unix in 1972. We end here.

Unit 11 asks what it means to read code that produces language. The answer requires going back to Usenet, to mailing lists, to SourceForge, to Stack Overflow — the successive commons that human knowledge has built, maintained, and sometimes handed to the wrong people. It requires reading *Attention Is All You Need* (Vaswani et al., 2017), the paper that changed what computation means. And it requires sitting with Andrej Karpathy's nanoGPT — 300 lines of Python that reproduce the core architecture — and asking: do you understand this?

The arc of the course ends in a question rather than an answer. We have read a compiler, an operating system, a proof assistant, a game engine, a browser. We have followed the logic of free software from Stallman to the GPL to the commons to the crisis of dependency. Now we read the thing that reads us.

Hinton leaves Google. Karpathy builds the readable version. Spolsky watches Stack Overflow displace the knowledge commons he built. The question is not whether LLMs are intelligent. The question is whether we are literate enough to have the conversation.
