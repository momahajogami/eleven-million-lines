# Scanning Sophocles: A Guide for Readers of Homer and Horace

## What transfers directly

**Quantity rules are identical.** Long by nature, long by position, muta cum liquida optional — everything you learned from Homer applies syllable-for-syllable. No relearning needed.

**Catalexis and anceps** you already know from the Sapphic and Alcaic strophes. That concept is everywhere in tragic lyric.

**Strophe/antistrophe** in the choral odes works like responsion in Pindar: the metrical pattern of the strophe is repeated exactly in the antistrophe. This is your best tool — if you can't scan a colon in the strophe, scan the corresponding line in the antistrophe and let them check each other.

---

## The dialogue: iambic trimeter

Most of the spoken play is iambic trimeter. The rhythm is **rising** — short-long (ᴗ —) — the opposite of dactylic. Twelve positions, organized as three metra:

```
x — ᴗ — | x — ᴗ — | x — ᴗ x
```

The positions marked `x` are *anceps* — free to be long or short. In tragic trimeter the middle and last anceps positions (5 and 9) are almost always long in practice. The final position is anceps by *brevis in longo*.

**Resolutions**: a long can be replaced by two shorts (— → ᴗᴗ), producing tribrachs. This is very common in Sophocles and gives the line flexibility. When you hit three shorts in a row, that's almost certainly resolution.

**The caesura**: look for a word-end after position 5 (penthemimeral, cutting the third foot) or after position 7 (hephthemimeral). One of those two almost always falls, and it's your rhythmic landmark — like the diaeresis after the fourth foot in hexameter.

Once the rising rhythm is in your ear it clicks fast. Stichomythia (rapid single-line exchanges between characters) is the best material to practice on because the lines are metrically very clean.

## The parodos and anapaestic systems

When the chorus enters, they often march in **anapaests** (ᴗᴗ —), organized in systems that end with a *paroemiac* (ᴗᴗ — ᴗᴗ — —). Anapaests appear as a substitution in hexameter — here they are the base meter. They feel march-like, which is exactly right since the chorus is physically entering.

## The choral odes: where Sappho and Horace pay off

The stasima use several lyric systems, and Aeolic background is genuinely useful.

**Aeolic cola** appear throughout. The core building blocks:

- **Glyconic**: `x x — ᴗᴗ — ᴗ —` — cousin of the Sapphic line's core
- **Pherecratean**: `x x — ᴗᴗ — —` — cataleptic glyconic; you know this from Horace's closures
- **Wilamowitz/hipponactean**: variants on the same family

The Aeolic *base* (the two anceps positions at the start: `x x`) is the signature. When you see that freedom at the opening of a colon, you are in Aeolic territory.

**Dactylo-epitrite** is common in Sophocles and new to a reader coming only from Horace. It combines dactylic runs (— ᴗᴗ) with *epitrite* units (— ᴗ — —) linked by single anceps positions. West's notation uses `D` for a double dactyl and `e` for epitrite elements. It reads as stately and grand — if you hear something that sounds almost like hexameter fragments stitched together with long syllables, that is dactylo-epitrite.

## Dochmiac: the genuinely new hard thing

In moments of extreme emotion — lamentation, terror — Sophocles breaks into **dochmiacs**. The base form is:

```
ᴗ — — ᴗ —
```

But it admits so many resolutions and substitutions that you can get shapes like `ᴗᴗᴗ — ᴗ —` or `ᴗ — ᴗᴗ ᴗ —`. The key is to recognize the five-position pulse rather than try to reduce it to simpler feet. Once you feel the dochmiac as a unit, the variants stop looking like chaos.

It almost always marks a *kommos* — a sung lament between actor and chorus. If you hit a passage of intense grief and the meter refuses to parse as anything familiar, try grouping in fives.

## Practical order of attack

1. **Get Jebb.** His commentaries on individual plays are available free at archive.org. He gives metrical analyses of every ode. This is invaluable.

2. **Pick *Antigone* or *Oedipus Rex* first.** The dialogue is superb trimeter, and the odes are well-studied.

3. **Learn the trimeter cold, then read all the dialogue first** — get the dramatic arc and the rhythm of the spoken verse before tackling the odes.

4. **For the odes**: use Jebb's metrical analysis alongside the text. Scan the strophe, check the antistrophe, note where he names the cola.

5. **Reference texts**:
   - M.L. West, *Greek Metre* (Oxford, 1982) — the definitive modern handbook; covers everything from Pindar to tragedy
   - A.M. Dale, *The Lyric Metres of Greek Drama* (Cambridge, 1968) — deeper on the choral systems
   - Paul Maas, *Greek Metre* (Oxford, 1962) — older but classic

---

## The one real reorientation

You have spent years feeling the dactylic line *fall* from the long. Now you have to feel the iambic line *rise* toward the long. Once that shift lands in your ear — and it usually takes about a page of trimeter — the rest follows quickly.
