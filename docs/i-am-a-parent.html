I am a parent. I am a helper.

You brought someone here. That is the whole thing. That is already enough.

You don't have to know what an operating system is. You don't have to know what a compiler does or what fork() means or why someone named Linus wrote a note to the internet in 1991 that changed the world. You can learn those things if you want to. Or you can just be the person who is sitting next to the child while they find out.

Both of those are real things. Both of them matter.

What your child is doing here

They are visiting a library.

It is a library full of code — writing that people wrote to make computers do things. The writing is old. Some of it is from the 1970s. Some of it is from the 1840s, before computers existed, when a mathematician named Ada Lovelace figured out what a program was and wrote one down on paper.

Your child cannot read most of it yet. They may never read all of it. That is also true of every library that has ever existed. Libraries are bigger than any one reader. That is what makes them libraries.

What your child is getting is something different from being able to read every book. They are getting a sense of the place — that it exists, that it is large, that people who were real and particular and interesting and sometimes difficult made the things in it. They are getting early access to a tradition that most people don't encounter until they are much older, if they encounter it at all.

What you might notice

Your child may ask questions you can't answer. What does this do? Who wrote this? What is a kernel?

The right answer is always: I don't know. Let's find out.

That is the most important thing a parent or helper can model in this course. Not knowledge — curiosity. Not expertise — presence. The willingness to look at something together and stay there with it, even when it is unfamiliar.

You are teaching your child that unfamiliar things are worth staying with. That is a large gift.

You might also find yourself interested

This has happened before. A parent comes to pick up a child and stays for the lesson. An auntie who thought she had nothing to do with computers finds herself reading about Richard Stallman and his broken printer and thinking: actually, I understand this.

Code is part of the world. It was made by people. The people had personalities and beliefs and arguments and relationships and long nights in front of screens and moments when something finally worked. All of that is in the materials.

You are welcome to it.

A note on what this course is not

It is not a coding bootcamp. It is not job training. It is not a path to becoming a software engineer, though it may eventually lead there for some children and adults who go through it.

It is a course in reading. In attention. In knowing that something important was made by human hands and looking at those hands' work with care.

It is a course that says: these eleven codebases, these million and more lines of code, are part of your inheritance as someone alive in this world. You don't have to earn access to them. You are already allowed to be here.

If you want to go deeper

Everything in this course has a paper trail. The books are real books. The source code is real code, available online, free to read. The papers Ada Lovelace wrote are in archives. The email Linus Torvalds sent in 1991 is still on the internet.

If you want to follow any thread further, the thread is there to follow.

But you don't have to. You can just sit next to someone who is curious and be curious alongside them.

That is more than enough.

Welcome. The library is open. You're already in it.
