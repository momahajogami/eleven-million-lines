Heritage — Unit 07: The Mathematics Beneath

Some people do mathematics the way other people breathe — not to accomplish something, but because stopping would be the strange thing.

Henri Poincaré (1854–1912)

He was the last person to understand all of mathematics.

This is not a romantic exaggeration. In the late nineteenth century, mathematics had not yet fractured into the mutually incomprehensible subspecialties it would become. Poincaré worked in complex analysis, celestial mechanics, topology, mathematical physics, and philosophy of science — not dabbling in each, but making foundational contributions to all of them. He discovered what we now call the Lorentz transformation before Lorentz. He described the special theory of relativity independently of Einstein (a fact that historians argue about). He proved the three-body problem has no general closed-form solution.

In 1895 he published Analysis Situs — the paper that founded algebraic topology. He wanted to understand the shapes of spaces: not just flat geometry, but the global structure of manifolds, the question of when two spaces are "the same" in some deep sense. He invented the concept of the fundamental group. He defined what we now call Betti numbers. He conjectured — but could not prove — that any simply connected, closed three-dimensional manifold is topologically equivalent to the three-sphere.

That conjecture took a hundred years to prove. Grigori Perelman posted the proof to arXiv in 2002 and 2003, then declined the Fields Medal and the million-dollar Millennium Prize. He said the proof belonged equally to Richard Hamilton, who had developed the method. He retreated to Saint Petersburg and stopped publishing.

The Poincaré conjecture is now the Poincaré theorem. The method used to prove it — Ricci flow with surgery — was not available to Poincaré, and may not have been conceivable to him. He had posed a problem that required tools invented a century after his death.

Poincaré wrote several popular books on mathematical intuition and the psychology of mathematical discovery. They are still in print. He said he often solved problems while unable to sleep, in the hypnagogic state between waking and sleep, when the unconscious made connections the deliberate mind had missed. He recommended going to sleep with a problem unsolved and reading the answer in the morning.

He died in 1912 from complications of prostate surgery. He was fifty-eight. Mathematics has not had a comparable generalist since.

Emmy Noether (1882–1935)

She invented abstract algebra.

Not a contribution to it — the field as practiced today, the field you are in when you write "let G be a group" or "let R be a ring" — that is the Noether framework. Before Noether, algebra dealt with specific objects: matrices, polynomials, numbers. Noether looked at the structure underlying all of them and gave it a name and a theory.

Her theorem — published in 1915, though she was still being denied a faculty position because she was a woman — establishes a correspondence between symmetries of a physical system and conservation laws. Every continuous symmetry produces a conserved quantity: time-translation symmetry produces conservation of energy; space-translation symmetry produces conservation of momentum; rotational symmetry produces conservation of angular momentum. Einstein called it the most important theorem in mathematical physics. It is taught in every graduate physics program. Its author was being lectured to by men of substantially lesser ability who held her degree for which she had been denied employment.

Hilbert fought for her. He could not understand why her sex should be relevant to the mathematics. He managed eventually to get her appointed as a Privatdozent — an unpaid teaching position, the lowest rung of the academic ladder. She taught. She produced results. She trained a generation of algebraists who spread her framework across the world.

In 1933 the Nazis dismissed her from her position. She was Jewish. She came to Bryn Mawr College in Pennsylvania, where she was given a professorship and, for the first time, paid a fair salary for her work. She died two years later, in 1935, of complications from surgery to remove an ovarian cyst. She was fifty-three.

Her students gave the eulogy. Einstein wrote an obituary in the New York Times. He called her the most significant creative mathematical genius thus far produced.

Thus far is the qualifier of a man who was careful with words.

Alexander Grothendieck (1928–2014)

His father was a Russian anarchist named Alexander "Sascha" Shapiro, who fought in the Russian Revolution on the wrong side, fled, fought in the Spanish Civil War, was interned in France by the Vichy government, and was transported to Auschwitz in 1942, where he was murdered. His mother, Hanka Grothendieck, was a German-Jewish radical writer who had left her husband and child in Hamburg to follow Sascha across Europe. Young Alexander spent years of his childhood in internment camps in southern France, stateless, holding no passport of any nation.

He taught himself mathematics. He read Lebesgue's work on measure theory at sixteen and reconstructed the theory independently, not knowing it already existed. When he arrived at university, the mathematicians he met recognized what they were dealing with.

By his late twenties, working at the Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques outside Paris, he was transforming algebraic geometry. Not improving the existing framework — replacing it. He introduced the notion of a scheme, which generalized the objects of algebraic geometry to include arithmetic in a way that had been impossible before. He developed topos theory — a framework that united topology and logic under the same umbrella. He introduced étale cohomology to prove the Weil conjectures (his student Pierre Deligne completed the final step in 1974). He ran the Séminaire de Géométrie Algébrique, known as SGA, a multi-year collaborative seminar whose written proceedings run to thousands of pages, produced by dozens of collaborators organized by Grothendieck.

In 1970, at forty-two, at the absolute height of his powers, he resigned from the IHÉS when he learned the institute received military funding. He had become increasingly concerned about militarism and environmental destruction. The mathematical world was shocked. He could not be replaced.

He gave several more lectures, organized a brief environmental activist group called Survivre, and then — gradually, then completely — withdrew. In the 1980s he wrote Récoltes et Semailles (Reaping and Sowing), a memoir of his mathematical life and his disillusionment with the mathematical community that had, he felt, adopted his ideas without understanding the spirit behind them. It runs to over a thousand pages. It is partly mathematical, partly confessional, partly accusatory. He sent copies to mathematicians he had worked with. Many found it disturbing.

In 1991 he moved to a small village in the Pyrenees and lived alone. He grew vegetables. He refused all contact with the mathematical world. When the Crafoord Prize was offered — 350,000 Swedish kronor — he refused it, saying that mathematicians already had sufficient resources. He refused to allow his unpublished manuscripts to be released.

He died in 2014. His neighbors did not know who he was.

His unpublished manuscripts, held in various archives, are slowly being digitized and studied. They contain, by all accounts, mathematics that nobody fully understands yet.

F. William Lawvere (b. 1937)

Lawvere is the least famous of the four people in this file and the most alive — as of this writing, he is still occasionally publishing, in his late eighties, working on the same project he began in the early 1960s.

The project: rebuild the foundations of mathematics using category theory.

He arrived at Columbia University in the early 1960s and attended Samuel Eilenberg's seminars. Category theory — invented by Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane in the 1940s to describe relationships between mathematical structures — was being developed in real time. Lawvere saw in it the seeds of something larger: a language capable of unifying logic, geometry, and computation.

His doctoral dissertation was rejected by his initial committee as insufficiently mathematical. The objection was that it was too concerned with foundations — with what mathematics is rather than what it does. He found other advisors and received his PhD. The dissertation is now recognized as one of the foundational documents of categorical logic.

He has been at SUNY Buffalo for most of his career. He works slowly and thinks for a long time before publishing. His papers are dense and foundational and repay careful reading more than any single reading can deliver.

He once described mathematics as a science of the structure of thought — not a collection of objects, but a collection of relationships between objects, and relationships between those relationships, going up. This is category theory. This is also, in some sense, what Grothendieck was building: not the objects of mathematics but the morphisms, the maps, the transformations.

What these four have in common

They were all working on the same question, from different angles, across different centuries: what is the deep structure of mathematical things?

Poincaré asked it about space. Noether asked it about algebraic operations. Grothendieck asked it about geometric objects. Lawvere asked it about mathematical theories themselves.

None of them was primarily interested in applying mathematics to engineering. They were interested in mathematics the way a poet is interested in language — not for the messages it can carry, but for the structure of the thing itself.

The code in this unit — Mathlib, Agda, the topology libraries — is the attempt to make that structure executable. The question underneath the code is: can we give the machine something true enough to work with?

The people who first asked that question did not have machines. They had paper, and chalk, and occasionally insufficient support from institutions that should have done better.
