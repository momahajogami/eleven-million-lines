Everything in Your Pocket

Social Media After Facebook

A narrative for Unit 10, part two

The Phone

On June 29, 2007, the first iPhone went on sale.

People had been lining up outside Apple Stores for days. This happened occasionally for game consoles; it had not happened for a phone. The line was not just enthusiasm — it was a premonition. Something was different about this one and people could feel it without being able to say exactly what.

What was different was the glass.

Previous smartphones had keyboards, styluses, small plastic buttons you pressed. The iPhone had a single sheet of glass and a finger. You touched it. It responded. You put two fingers on a photo and spread them apart and the photo got larger. You pinched them together and it shrank. This was called pinch to zoom and the description does not convey the experience of encountering it for the first time. It felt like a trick. It felt like the screen understood what you wanted. People in Apple Stores stood and pinched and zoomed and laughed, the way you laugh when a magician does something that shouldn't be possible.

This sounds trivial. It was not. The tactile interface — the sense that you were touching the content rather than operating a device that contained the content — changed the relationship between a person and a computer. The desktop computer had always been across the desk. The laptop had always been open in front of you. The iPhone was in your hand. Your hand. The same hand that held food and tools and other people's hands.

Steve Jobs called it "the internet in your pocket." That was the marketing. The reality was more intimate: yourself online, in your pocket. Not at a desk. Not when you got home. Always.

The Practical Magic

Before the social consequences, there were the practical ones, and they were extraordinary.

Maps. Google Maps on the iPhone, with GPS, meant you were always a few seconds from knowing exactly where you were and how to get anywhere. This sounds like a small convenience. For anyone who remembers navigating a city with a paper map, or printing directions, or asking strangers, or just getting lost for an hour: it was not small. It was the elimination of a whole category of anxiety. You could arrive in a city you had never visited and orient yourself immediately. You could find the nearest subway station, the nearest pharmacy, the route home from wherever you ended up.

Public transit. Transit apps — knowing in real time when the next bus would arrive, whether the train was running, which exit to take — changed urban life in specific and practical ways that are easy to undervalue. For anyone dependent on public transit to get to work, school, medical appointments: the difference between guessing and knowing is significant. The phone made transit legible.

The camera. The iPhone camera was not the best camera you could buy in 2007. But it was the camera you always had with you, and it connected instantly to everything else. You took a photo and it was already in your pocket alongside your contacts and your messages. The friction between seeing something and sharing it approached zero. This changed what people photographed. Not just events — just life. Food and clouds and funny signs and the view from the window and the face of a friend across a table.

People started texting each other photos. Not posting — texting. MMS, then iMessage, then WhatsApp. A conversation that would have been words became images, became a visual language between two people. This is a new kind of writing. It does not have a name yet. The grammar is still being worked out.

Two Things Evolving at Once

Here is what was happening, in parallel, in the years after 2007:

Smartphones were being invented. Every year, the camera got better, the screen got larger, the processor got faster, the battery got worse and then better. The App Store opened in 2008 and within weeks had tens of thousands of applications. Android launched the same year, running on every phone that wasn't an iPhone, fragmenting the market into two camps that still exist.

Social applications were being invented. Each one was an experiment in a specific kind of human need: the need to broadcast briefly, to share images, to disappear, to be seen by strangers, to be seen only by friends, to perform, to witness.

These two evolutions — the hardware and the software — were not independent. Each one made the other more possible. The better camera made Instagram meaningful. The larger screen made video watchable. The GPS made location-based features real. The social apps, in turn, gave people reasons to upgrade their phones, to care about camera quality, to want faster processors.

This parallel evolution is not tidy. It does not have a single story. What it has is a series of moments when something new appeared and changed what people thought social media was for.

Twitter

Twitter launched in 2006 as an internal tool at a podcasting company called Odeo. Jack Dorsey's original pitch was simple: what are you doing right now? A status update, limited to 140 characters — the SMS limit, minus room for a username.

The constraint turned out to be the form. 140 characters is long enough to say something and short enough to demand that you mean it. Twitter found its voice in a series of discoveries: the @reply (2006, invented by users, not the company), the hashtag (2007, same), the retweet (2009, same). The core social gestures of the platform were not designed — they emerged from use and were then formalized.

Twitter was not a friend network. It was a broadcast network with asymmetric following: you could follow anyone, and anyone could follow you, without mutual consent. This made it something different from Facebook. Facebook was your life. Twitter was the public square. You could listen to people you'd never meet. You could talk and anyone could hear you.

The Arab Spring (2010-2012) made Twitter feel historic. Protests in Tunisia, Egypt, Libya, Syria — organizers used Twitter to coordinate, journalists used it to report in real time, the world used it to watch. Whether Twitter caused the Arab Spring or merely carried its signal was contested, and that contestation was also conducted on Twitter. The platform had become a space where events and the conversation about events happened simultaneously.

Twitter's technical story is a scaling saga. The "fail whale" — the illustration of a whale being lifted by birds that appeared when Twitter was down, which was often — became a cultural artifact. The early stack was Ruby on Rails, chosen for development speed, and it couldn't handle the load. The rewrite was painful and took years. The engineers who lived through it wrote candidly about it, and those posts are worth reading.

Twitter never solved its moderation problem. Harassment was endemic from early on; the platform's architecture — public by default, anonymous by choice, global reach — made it structurally difficult to protect users from coordinated abuse. The company's responses were slow, inconsistent, and often inadequate. This is not incidental. The design choices that made Twitter powerful also made it dangerous, and untangling one from the other turned out to be harder than the company expected or admitted.

Instagram

Instagram launched in October 2010 as an iPhone-only app. The founding insight was small and correct: photos taken on phones looked bad. They were overexposed, underlit, flat. Filters — digital processing that mimicked the look of analog photography, of aged film, of particular vintight aesthetics — made them look better, or at least more intentional. The filter was permission: your ordinary photo, run through Valencia or X-Pro II, looked like you meant it.

The other insight was the square. Instagram cropped photos to a square format, echoing Polaroid and Holga cameras. The constraint was aesthetic and social: the square implies that you are showing a moment, not a composition. It is the size of a memory, not a painting.

Instagram launched with 25,000 users on its first day. Within a year it had 10 million. In April 2012, Facebook acquired it for approximately $1 billion — a number that seemed extraordinary at the time and looks modest now. At the time of acquisition, Instagram had 13 employees.

Instagram started as a photographer's app and became a broadcaster's platform. The shift was gradual and then sudden: the "influencer" — a person who built an audience on Instagram by projecting a lifestyle — emerged as a new category of celebrity. The influencer economy is built on a simple mechanism: enough followers, and brands pay you to show their product in your life. The life becomes content. The content becomes commerce.

Instagram Stories (2016), copied directly from Snapchat, changed the platform again: ephemeral content, 24 hours and gone. The permanent grid — the curated, composed, filtered presentation of your life — sat alongside the temporary story, the casual and impermanent daily dispatch. Two registers of self-presentation on the same app: the museum and the diary.

Snapchat

Snapchat (2011) was invented by Evan Spiegel and Bobby Murphy at Stanford. The founding idea was ephemerality: a photo or video sent through Snapchat would disappear after a few seconds. The recipient saw it, and then it was gone.

The intention, stated by Spiegel, was freedom. The permanent record of Facebook — the post that existed forever, the tag you couldn't fully remove, the archive of everything you had ever been — created a kind of performative pressure. Every post was a document. Snapchat proposed the opposite: a photo that vanished freed you to be more honest, more silly, more yourself, because there was no record.

This was widely and pruriously misunderstood as a technology for sending explicit photos (the photos did not actually disappear in any technically reliable sense; screenshots were possible). But the underlying design instinct was real: permanence changes behavior. People perform for archives. They are different when the archive is absent.

Snapchat caught on immediately among teenagers in a way that surprised and alarmed the adults who noticed. By 2013, Facebook had noticed, and offered to acquire Snapchat for $3 billion. Spiegel declined, at age 23. Facebook built Instagram Stories in response. The gesture was large enough to confirm that Snapchat had found something real.

The Snap Map — showing the real-time location of your friends on a map — was introduced in 2017. It was both useful and unsettling, depending on who you were and who was watching.

Vine

Vine was a six-second looping video app acquired by Twitter in 2012, before it launched, for $30 million. Six seconds. A loop that played forever until you swiped away.

The constraint, as always, was the form. Six seconds is not enough time for a story in the traditional sense. It is exactly enough time for a joke, a gesture, a visual gag, a moment of perfect timing. Vine created a generation of creators who learned to work in six-second bursts — comedians, dancers, athletes, absurdists. The format demanded economy. Every frame had to earn its place.

King Bach, Zach King, Brittany Furlan, Thomas Sanders: Vine stars who built audiences of millions before Instagram or YouTube gave them larger canvases. The format was so specific that moving to another platform was not always a translation — it was a reinvention.

Twitter shut Vine down in October 2016. The stated reason was profitability. The actual reason was complicated: Twitter didn't know how to monetize it, and the top creators were asking for revenue-sharing arrangements that Twitter wasn't prepared to offer. The closure was announced without ceremony. Six-second loops vanished from the internet. The creators moved to YouTube and Instagram.

People still talk about Vine the way people talk about places that no longer exist. Not with nostalgia exactly — with a specific grief for something that worked and was ended for reasons that had nothing to do with whether it worked.

TikTok, which launched internationally in 2018, is Vine's spiritual successor: short looping video, algorithmic discovery, a new generation of creators learning a new constraint. The cycle continues.

The Generation Lines

Every platform has an age. Not its age in years — the age of the people on it.

Facebook opened to everyone in 2006. By 2010, parents were on Facebook. By 2012, grandparents. The effect on teenage usage was immediate and measurable: Facebook became the place where your family could see what you were doing. The young fled.

Where they went depended on when they were born.

The early millennials went to Tumblr and Twitter. The late millennials went to Instagram. The early Gen Z went to Snapchat. The late Gen Z went to TikTok. Each migration carried a logic: this is the place where adults are not yet watching, where we can be ourselves, where the norms are ours and not inherited.

The platforms that survived this pattern survived by becoming infrastructure — by being too useful to leave even when they became surveilled. Facebook survived because your relatives and your invitations and your event pages were on it. Instagram survived because it remained the dominant space for visual identity. Twitter survived because certain professional and political conversations happened nowhere else.

The ones that didn't survive either lost the young and couldn't replace them (Vine, Tumblr) or were bought and altered until they became something else (LiveJournal, MySpace).

The generational divide is not just about which apps people use. It is about what communication is. For people who came of age before smartphones, a phone call is a normal thing; for many people who came of age after, an unannounced phone call is an intrusion. The voicemail is an anachronism. The DM is the letter. The meme is the joke. These are not equivalent translations — they are different forms, carrying different social meanings, and the gap between generations is partly a gap between people who learned different forms at the age when forms are learned.

If you are not in, you are out. This is not a metaphor. Teenagers who are not on the platform where their social life happens are actually absent from their social life. The conversation that happened in Snapchat stories, the plan made in an Instagram group DM, the meme that everyone referenced for a week — if you weren't there, you missed it, and the missing is not recoverable. There is no "catching up" the way there was no catching up on a conversation that happened when you weren't in the room.

The Last Time You Borrowed Someone's Phone

There was a period when borrowing a phone was possible. You were out, your battery was dead, and you asked a stranger or a friend to borrow their phone for a minute. You made a call. You handed it back.

The smartphone ended this. Not because people became less generous — because the phone became too personal to lend. Your entire financial life is in that phone. Your medical records. Your private messages. Your location history. Your browser history. Your photos. Every authentication token for every account. The phone is not a phone; it is you, as a data object, with a battery and a screen.

The net café is gone. The public library terminal — still there, but no longer the primary means of access for anyone who has a choice. The digital divide, which was once a divide between people who had computers and people who didn't, is now a divide between people who have good phones and people who have bad ones, between people with unlimited data and people who ration it.

The infrastructure of public access to the internet — the library terminal, the net café, the school computer lab as primary point of access — existed because the internet was somewhere you went. The smartphone made the internet somewhere you were. This is a liberation and a class marker simultaneously.

What All These Apps Are

Here is an honest accounting of what happened:

The desire to be seen — to be present to people who matter to you, to have a place online that is yours — was real and is real. The first wave of social platforms found this desire and responded to it clumsily, charmingly, experimentally. Facebook organized it into infrastructure. The smartphone put the infrastructure in your body.

What came after was an ecosystem, not a plan. Twitter became the public square and the harassment engine simultaneously, because the architecture that enables one enables the other. Instagram became the space for visual identity and the engine of the influencer economy simultaneously, because those two things want the same features. Snapchat became the place for ephemeral honesty and for anxiety about who watched your story simultaneously, because the counter that shows who viewed is right there.

Each of these is not a unified thing. It is a negotiation between what the platform designed, what users wanted, and what the advertising model required. The three parties have different interests and the platform is the site of their conflict.

There is no tidy story. The people who lived through it experienced it differently by age, by geography, by which platform found them first, by what they were looking for when they arrived. A forty-year-old in 2010 had a different Facebook than a seventeen-year-old. A creator with a hundred thousand Instagram followers had a different Instagram than someone who used it to keep up with family photos.

We are not going to pretend otherwise. The honest orientation is: this is what happened, approximately, in rough sequence, to some people in some places. The complexity is the point. The diversity of experience is the point. The fact that your version of this story may not match the version told here is not an error — it is the subject.

An Invitation to the Code

All of this — every like button, every notification badge, every algorithmic feed, every ephemeral story — is engineering.

Someone designed the data structure. Someone wrote the database schema. Someone decided how the follower graph would be stored, how the feed would be generated, how the engagement metric would be defined, which metric the algorithm would optimize for. Someone ran the A/B test that proved the infinite scroll increased time-on-app. Someone implemented it.

These decisions were not made in a vacuum. They were made under business pressure, under competitive pressure, under deadlines, with imperfect information, by people who were often very smart and sometimes very young and almost never thinking primarily about what effect their decision would have on the emotional life of a teenager in rural Ohio.

The code is not separate from the consequences. The code is the consequences, specified in advance and deployed at scale.

Reading the code is reading the decision. The Haystack paper — how Facebook stores photos — is a decision about what photos are and what they're for. The Twitter infrastructure posts from 2009 — why the fanout model failed — are a decision about what it means to follow someone. The React Native architecture — the framework Facebook built to write mobile apps once and run them on both iPhone and Android — is a decision about what the phone is and who gets to build for it.

This is why we are here: not to assign blame or celebrate innovation or trace the arc of history to its inevitable conclusion. We are here because the code exists, it is readable, and reading it is one of the most direct ways available to understand how the world you are living in was made.

The browser made it possible. The search engine made it findable. The social network made it personal. The smartphone made it constant. The code made all of it real.

Open a file. Read it slowly. Find out who decided.
