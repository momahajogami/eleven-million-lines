The Desire to Be Seen

Social Media Before Facebook

A narrative for Unit 10

The Feeling First

Before the technology, there was the feeling.

You were online. You had a connection — dial-up, maybe broadband by the early 2000s. You could reach servers anywhere in the world. You could read news, download software, look up anything. The internet was enormous and getting larger every day.

And you could not see your friends.

This sounds small. It wasn't. The people you knew — your actual people, the ones you ate lunch with and called on the phone and thought about — were not there. The internet was full of strangers and information and commerce, and the people who mattered to you were absent from it entirely. You couldn't see what they were doing. You couldn't see what they looked like, in the way a photo shows you. You couldn't make a profile. You couldn't say: this is me, here I am, this is what I care about.

The desire to be seen — to have a presence, to exist online in a way that connected to your actual life — was real and had nowhere to go. It built pressure through the 1990s. The technologies that eventually released that pressure were not built to release it. They stumbled into it.

Email and the First Proof

Email was the first thing that worked.

Not web-based email — not yet. First, email on university servers, then on ISP accounts (CompuServe, AOL, eventually Hotmail). You had an address. The address was yours. If you gave it to someone and they gave you theirs, you could write to them directly, across any distance, for free.

This is not nothing. Before email, staying in touch with people required effort proportional to distance. A letter took days and cost a stamp. A long-distance phone call cost real money, charged by the minute. People lost touch because staying in touch was expensive. Email made it free and nearly instant.

The effect on friendship was quiet but cumulative. You could write to a college friend who had moved away. You could keep a conversation going with someone you'd met briefly and wanted to know better. The social graph — the actual network of your relationships — didn't have to contract when geography changed. It could stay open.

But email was private. It was one-to-one, or one-to-mailing-list. It was not a presence. Receiving an email from someone told you they thought of you in that moment; it did not tell you who they were becoming, what they were doing, what their life looked like day to day. You still couldn't see them.

Early Radio

No one knew what the internet was for.

This is important to say plainly, because looking back it is easy to see the arc — BBSs to Usenet to the web to social media — as inevitable, as if the destination was always visible. It was not. The people building in the 1990s and early 2000s were genuinely experimental. They were trying things. Most of the things didn't work.

The analogy that fits is early radio. When broadcasting began in the 1920s, nobody knew what radio was for. The first stations played music, read poetry, broadcast sermons, transmitted weather reports, let people talk about whatever they wanted. A farmer in Iowa could speak and be heard in Chicago. This was extraordinary and nobody knew what to do with it. The first commercial radio station, KDKA in Pittsburgh, broadcast the 1920 presidential election results as its inaugural program, not because election results were the obvious use of radio, but because someone had to broadcast something and that was what was happening.

The form took a decade to find itself: the variety show, the soap opera, the news broadcast, the comedy hour. These formats were not obvious in advance. They emerged from experiment, from finding out what people wanted to hear, from the interaction between the medium and the audience.

The early social web was in the same phase. Nobody knew what it was for. You could try anything.

Forums and the Proto-Self

Before profiles, there were forums.

Forums were threaded discussion boards: topics, replies, nested conversations. phpBB, vBulletin, Simple Machines. Every subculture had forums. Skateboarding forums, music forums, forums for every band and sports team and TV show. Forums for people who had specific medical conditions, forums for people in specific cities, forums for people who liked specific kinds of coffee.

The forum gave you a username and an avatar — a small image, usually 100 by 100 pixels — and a post count and a signature. These were crude instruments for self-expression, but they were instruments. Your username was a name you chose. Your avatar was something you selected to represent you. Your signature could contain a quote you loved or a link to something you made. Your post count was a measure of your investment in the community.

People cared about these things. They agonized over usernames. They changed avatars seasonally. A high post count was social capital. You were somebody in this forum if you had been here long enough and said enough true things.

But the forum was vertical. You were somebody here, in this specific community, on this specific topic. You weren't yourself — you weren't the full, cross-cutting, complicated actual person — you were your forum self, which was real but partial. There was no place online where all of you was present.

Friendster and the Proof of Concept

Friendster launched in 2002. Jonathan Abrams built it in his apartment in San Francisco. The idea was simple: make your social network visible. You created a profile — a real profile, with your name and your photo and some things about yourself — and you connected it to profiles of people you actually knew. Their profiles linked to profiles of people they knew. You could navigate the network. You could see who knew whom. You could see that your friend from college knew your coworker, that you were three steps from someone you wanted to meet.

The name was the argument: a portmanteau of "friend" and "Napster." Napster had used peer-to-peer networking to share music; Friendster would use social networking to share people.

It worked. Within a few months of launch, Friendster had a million users. Within a year, it had several million. Google offered to acquire it for $30 million in 2003. Abrams declined, believing Friendster was worth more.

Then the servers fell over.

Friendster's infrastructure could not handle the load. The database queries for social graph traversal — "show me everything about this person, and everyone connected to them, and rate of interaction, and..." — were slow and getting slower as the network grew. Page loads took thirty seconds. Then sixty. Users waited and grew frustrated and left.

This is the Friendster lesson: a social network is a distributed systems problem. The engineering is not a feature — it is the foundation. Without the engineering, the idea doesn't matter.

Friendster never recovered from the performance problems. It survived in Southeast Asia, where it remained popular until 2011, and then shut down. Abrams's decision not to sell to Google is often cited as one of the great missed opportunities in Silicon Valley history.

MySpace and the Bedroom Wall

MySpace launched in August 2003, built in ten days by a team at eUniverse (later Intermix Media) after watching Friendster's success. The founders — Tom Anderson, Chris DeWolfe, and others — were not building something philosophically distinct from Friendster. They were building Friendster faster, for the people Friendster was failing.

What made MySpace different was almost an accident: you could edit your profile with HTML and CSS.
