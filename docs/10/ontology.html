What Is a Thing?

Computing, Writing, and the Ontology of the Made-Up

A philosophical essay for Unit 10

The Repetition

By the time you reach Unit 10, you have seen this story three times.

In Unit 02, you read the source code of the C compiler that built the operating system that built every operating system since. You learned that in 1976, when this work was being done — mostly by amateurs, hobbyists, academics, people who did it because the problem was interesting — a twenty-year-old named Bill Gates wrote a letter to those hobbyists and told them they were thieves. They were using software without paying for it. This was harming the industry. If people didn't pay for software, there would be no incentive to write good software, and the software would be bad, and everyone would suffer.

In Unit 03, you met Richard Stallman, who in 1983 announced that he was going to write a free operating system — free as in freedom, not free as in zero cost, though it was that too — because he had watched the principle Gates was asserting become policy, and he thought the principle was wrong. Not wrong as in mistaken. Wrong as in immoral. Software that you cannot read, cannot modify, cannot share, cannot build on: Stallman called this an injustice. He spent forty years making the case.

In Unit 05, you watched the browser wars. Microsoft bundled Internet Explorer with Windows, making Netscape unable to compete with free. The Department of Justice said this was an illegal abuse of monopoly power. The courts mostly agreed. Microsoft settled and changed almost nothing. By the end of it, the commercial category of "software" — the thing you bought in a box at CompUSA, the thing the Open Letter was protecting — had quietly disappeared. Nobody sells you a word processor anymore. You subscribe. You license. You pay for the service, not the thing.

This is the third time. By now you know the shape of it. Now we can ask what the shape means.

What Gates Was Saying

The Open Letter to Hobbyists is a founding document of the computing era. It is worth reading carefully, because it contains an argument that most people accepted without examination and that the next fifty years then demonstrated to be false.

Gates's argument was economic: software takes work to write; work costs money; if people use the software without paying, the writer is not compensated; if the writer is not compensated, the writer will not write; therefore piracy harms everyone by reducing the incentive to produce software.

This is a reasonable argument for some things. It is the argument for why you pay for bread: someone grew the wheat, someone milled it, someone baked it, you eat it, if you eat it without paying the baker doesn't eat. The bread is consumed. It is gone. There is one fewer loaf.

Software is not bread.

When you copy software, the original is still there. Nothing is consumed. The copying costs essentially nothing — in 1976 it cost the price of a floppy disk; now it costs nothing measurable. Gates was asserting property rights over something that does not behave like property. He was asking you to pay for a copy of a pattern, not for the pattern itself, not for the work that created the pattern, and not for any continuing service. You paid once, you got a disk, the disk had software on it, and Microsoft's involvement in your life was over.

There was not a thing there. There was never a thing there. What you were paying for was permission to use a pattern that Microsoft would continue to own. The pattern lived in your computer. Microsoft held the legal right to the pattern. You held a license.

Gates understood this. His letter does not claim you are stealing bread. It claims you are stealing his labor — the "royalty" he is owed for the work of creation. But a royalty is not a property right in a thing. It is an ongoing claim on future revenue derived from a work. The conceptual slippage — between owning a thing, owning the right to a pattern, and being owed compensation for creative work — is doing enormous work in that letter, and it was doing enormous work in the law and the economy for the next fifty years.

What Gates was really establishing was not that software is property. It is that the legal fiction of software as property is profitable, and that he would enforce it.

What Stallman Was Saying

Stallman's response is philosophically cleaner, which is why it is harder to explain.

He was not saying software should be free because it doesn't cost anything to copy. He was saying software should be free because software is instructions, and the right to share, modify, and build on instructions is a precondition of a functioning community of knowledge.

He drew an analogy to recipes. A chef does not give you a recipe for free because recipes are worthless. A chef gives you a recipe because the recipe is an artifact of knowledge that exists to be used and improved. A recipe you cannot share is a recipe that dies with you. A recipe you cannot modify is a recipe you cannot make better. The knowledge exists in the sharing.

Code is a recipe for a machine. Not a metaphor — a recipe. It is a precise set of instructions, written in a language, that tells a particular kind of machine what to do. The history of human knowledge is the history of sharing recipes. We called the sharing of recipes "teaching," "publishing," "science." The claim that you could own a recipe and prevent others from using it or improving it was, to Stallman, not a property right — it was an act of theft from the commons.

Stallman's legal instrument — the GPL, the General Public License — is an attempt to use copyright law against itself. You have a copyright on your software. The GPL requires that anyone who uses your software and distributes their own work built on it must distribute that work under the same terms. The license propagates. It grows. The commons expands automatically with every use. This is what Stallman called copyleft.

The argument between Gates and Stallman is not an argument about economics. It is an argument about what software is. Is it a product — a thing you make and sell? Or is it an artifact of knowledge — a text that exists to be read, used, improved, and transmitted?

This course is built on Stallman's answer. Not because we agree with all of his conclusions. Because the course is about reading code, and you cannot read what you are not permitted to open.

The Falsification

Gates said: if people don't pay for software, there will be no incentive to write good software.

The hobbyists kept writing anyway. The academics kept writing anyway. The people who found the problems interesting kept writing anyway.

By the time the web existed, the infrastructure that ran it — the servers, the compilers, the operating systems, the networking stack — was almost entirely open source. Linux ran (and runs) the majority of servers on the internet. Apache served the majority of web pages. GCC compiled most of the code. None of this was written by companies. Most of it was written by people who were not paid to write it, or who were paid by organizations (universities, research labs, companies that used the software) for whom the software was a means, not a product.

The technical quality of this work was extraordinary. Linux is not a hobby operating system. GCC is not a toy compiler. Apache is not an amateur web server. These are production-grade, battle-tested systems that run the global economy. They were written, largely, by people Gates would have called hobbyists.

Meanwhile, what did Microsoft produce with its protected, paid, proprietary model? Windows ME. Internet Explorer 6. The registry. The blue screen of death. Not bad products, some of them — but not the flood of great software that the protection of intellectual property was supposed to produce.

We do not hear from Microsoft about why the flood did not arrive. The argument was simply abandoned, the way bad arguments are abandoned when they are no longer needed. The commercial software model survived not because it produced better software — in many cases it produced worse — but because it was profitable, and profitability is a kind of inertia.

Gates was wrong about the incentive. People write software for the same reasons they write anything: because the problem interests them, because they want to communicate something, because they want to make something that works, because they want to impress their peers, because they believe in what they are building. The paycheck helps. It is not the engine.

Software Is Made Up

Here is the statement plainly: software is not a thing. It is a pattern. It is a text. It is instructions written in a language that machines can execute.

When you pay for software, you are not paying for a thing in the way that you pay for a car or a chair. You are paying for permission to use a pattern that someone else wrote down. The car wears out. The chair breaks. The pattern does not change when you use it. It does not diminish. It does not get used up.

This is not a loophole. It is the nature of the medium.

Consider: the number 3 is not a thing. You cannot own it. You cannot sell it. You can write it on paper, and the paper is a thing, and you can sell the paper. But the number 3 itself is not for sale. It predates any seller and will outlast any buyer.

Code is closer to 3 than it is to a chair. The specific expression of an algorithm — the exact sequence of characters in a particular source file — can be protected by copyright, the way the specific expression of a novel can be protected. But the algorithm itself — the sequence of steps, the logical structure, the idea — cannot be owned. Mathematics cannot be patented. Logic cannot be owned.

What Gates was protecting was the expression. What he was claiming to protect was the thing. The slippage between these two claims is where fifty years of technology law happened.

NFTs as the Reductio

In 2021, people started paying large sums of money for NFTs — Non-Fungible Tokens. You paid money, you received a token on a blockchain, and the token pointed to a JPEG. You did not own the JPEG. The JPEG was still publicly accessible to anyone. Anyone could right-click it and save it. You owned a record on a distributed ledger that said you owned something — a certificate of ownership for a thing that could not actually be owned.

People spent hundreds of thousands of dollars on these.

This is not an anomaly. This is the software model carried to its logical conclusion. When Gates sold you Windows in 1990, you did not own Windows. You owned a license. The license was a certificate of permission to use something that Microsoft continued to own. The floppy disk was physical. The software on it was not. You were paying for a pattern and receiving a certificate.

NFTs removed the floppy disk. They made the certificate the product. The result was absurd and obvious, and millions of people paid for it anyway, because the entire economy of software had been built on exactly this logic for fifty years, and people had been trained to treat it as normal.

The normal thing and the absurd thing are the same thing. The NFT made it visible.

The Layers

Personal computing created the condition. Social media built on it. The gig economy built on that.

Personal computing established that software was property. That you needed permission to use the tools that ran on your machine. That your computer was yours but the programs that made it useful were not. This created an economy of permission — a layer of legal and financial obligation between you and the tools you needed to do things.

Social media took the permission economy and inverted it. You no longer paid for the software. The software was free. You paid with your data, your attention, your social graph, your behavioral patterns. Facebook did not sell you a product. Facebook sold you to advertisers. The platform was the mechanism. Your life was the material.

This is a new kind of ontology. In the Microsoft era, you paid for something that wasn't quite a thing. In the Facebook era, the thing you were paying with wasn't quite yours. Your attention, your network, your identity as expressed through clicks and posts and reactions — these were captured and packaged and sold. The transaction happened, but you were not the buyer and not the seller. You were the product.

The gig economy added a third layer: your labor, disaggregated and resold.

Uber did not employ drivers. It licensed software to drivers and licensed access to a market. The driver provided the car, the fuel, the insurance, the labor. Uber provided the algorithm that matched them with a rider and the platform that processed the payment. The driver was not an employee — they were an independent contractor. The labor was theirs. The platform was Uber's. The value of the network — the fact that enough drivers meant faster pickups, which meant more riders, which meant more drivers — accrued entirely to Uber.

This is exactly the structure of the Open Letter to Hobbyists, one layer up. Gates said: we provide the platform (the programming environment, the operating system, the tools); you provide the labor (the code you write using them); we own the platform and you owe us for using it. Uber said: we provide the platform (the app, the market, the pricing algorithm); you provide the labor (the driving); we own the platform and you owe us for access to it.

The hobbyist who wrote software in 1976 and the Uber driver in 2016 are in structurally identical positions. Their labor creates value that someone else captures, through a legal fiction that designates the platform as property and the labor as expendable.

The Brain Snipping

There was a moment. Call it the moment before it closed.

In the late 1970s and early 1980s, personal computers were being made by hobbyists, for hobbyists. The Homebrew Computer Club met in a garage in Menlo Park. People brought things they had made. They showed each other. They shared schematics and code. The ethos was the ethos of amateur radio, of the gentleman scientist, of the GI Bill engineer — the idea that technology is a civic resource, that knowledge is for sharing, that the intelligent amateur has a right and a responsibility to participate.

This is the moment that Stallman was trying to preserve. When he saw the walls going up — when he could no longer get the printer's source code, when his colleagues started signing non-disclosure agreements, when the hobbyist clubs started asking about licenses — he recognized it as a closure. Something was being taken.

What was being taken was not code. It was a relationship to knowledge.

The computer was the most powerful reading and writing tool that had ever existed. It could make a person literate in mathematics, in logic, in process, in system. It could externalize thought in ways that no previous tool had achieved. It could be a prosthetic for intelligence, for memory, for the kinds of systematic reasoning that formal education had always rationed by class and opportunity.

It was being locked. Not physically — the machine sat on your desk. But the programs that animated it were being made opaque, proprietary, unchallengeable. You could use the tool but not understand it. You could run the program but not read it. You could buy the product but not improve it.

This is what we mean by brain snipping. The tool arrived at exactly the moment when it could have been most transformative — when computers were still simple enough to understand, when the source code fit in a book, when reading the OS was something a dedicated amateur could do in a week — and it was closed, just then, just as the possibility was becoming real.

The GI Bill had created a generation of educated veterans. The interstate highway system had been built by public money for public use. NASA had put men on the moon and given the technology away. The tradition of public investment in shared infrastructure was real and functional.

We did not build the internet that way. We built it, largely, with public money and open standards and volunteer labor — and then handed the infrastructure to private companies to own and monetize. The roads were public. The cars were public. The drivers were told they were contractors.

Civics and Computing

There is an analogy that is not quite an analogy.

The enterprise of civics is to the free creative individual as the enterprise of computing is to the personal computer.

Civics is the set of structures, agreements, and obligations that make it possible for individuals to live together freely. It is not opposed to individual freedom — it is the precondition for it. You can speak freely because there are courts that protect speech. You can walk freely because there are roads that are public. You can publish freely because there are laws that prevent anyone from owning the alphabet. The civic infrastructure is what freedom runs on.

Computing, properly understood, is the same structure. The open standards — TCP/IP, HTTP, HTML, Unicode — are the civic infrastructure of the digital world. They are what makes it possible for anyone to build anything and have it work. You can write a web server that serves everyone because the protocol is public. You can write a browser that reads everything because the standard is open. The infrastructure is what freedom runs on.

The proprietary software model was an attempt to privatize the civic infrastructure. Not all of it — the internet protocols survived, because they were too embedded to dislodge. But the layer above the protocols: the operating systems, the applications, the platforms. That layer was privatized.

The consequence was not just economic. It was cognitive. A citizen who cannot read the laws that govern them is not free — they are managed. A user who cannot read the code that runs their tools is not empowered — they are captured. Civics without literacy is compliance. Computing without literacy is consumption.

This is why this course exists. Not to train programmers. To train readers. To insist that the code is readable, that reading it is a civic act, that the tradition of doing so goes back to the beginning, and that it was interrupted by a legal fiction that is slowly losing its grip.

Why It Matters Now

We are in a third moment of the same story.

Large language models are trained on text — enormous quantities of text, including enormous quantities of code. The model learns the patterns. It can reproduce them, recombine them, extend them, simulate understanding. The model is, in a precise sense, a compression of the written record of human knowledge.

Who owns the model?

Not the people who wrote the text it was trained on. Not the programmers whose code it can reproduce. Not the users who interact with it. The model is owned by the companies that trained it. The labor — the decades of writing and coding and thinking — was the material. The platform is the product. The structure is identical to everything that came before.

We are being told, again, that this is the natural order. That the capital required to train these models is so large that only corporations can provide it, and therefore corporations must own the result. That the hobbyist cannot do this work, that the academic cannot do this work, that public infrastructure cannot do this work.

Gates said it about software in 1976. It was not true.

The question is not whether to have LLMs. They exist; they are useful; they are, in some ways, extraordinary. The question is what we are again agreeing to call property, and what we are again agreeing not to read.

Coding Is Writing

The last thing to say, and the simplest.

Coding is writing. This is not a metaphor. It is not an analogy designed to make programming accessible to the liberal arts student. It is a historical and technological fact.

Writing is the technology of externalizing thought into a medium that can be transmitted, stored, and retrieved. It began with clay tablets and reed styluses in Mesopotamia around 3200 BCE. It continued with papyrus, parchment, paper, the printing press, the typewriter, the word processor. Each new medium changed what writing could do. The printing press made copying cheap. The word processor made revision cheap. The internet made distribution free.

Code is writing in a medium that can be executed. The medium is the computer. The reader is the machine. But the author is a person, using language, making choices about structure and expression, trying to communicate something — to the machine, to other programmers, to posterity. The source code of xv6 is a text. The comment in fork() that says // it's already there is a sentence. Carmack's .plan files are a diary.

The history of computing is continuous with the history of writing. The Jacquard loom used punched cards to encode patterns for silk weaving in 1801. Ada Lovelace wrote the first program in 1843, in footnotes to a translation of someone else's paper. The tradition of people writing instructions for machines — and of machines that read — is older than the transistor, older than the vacuum tube, older than electricity.

This course is not a computer science course. It is a reading course. It is about the tradition of writing that includes code, and the tradition of reading that includes reading code, and the argument that separating these — that treating code as something other than writing, as something only specialists can read, as something owned rather than shared — is a mistake with consequences we are still living inside.

You already know how to read. We are just showing you more of what reading is.
