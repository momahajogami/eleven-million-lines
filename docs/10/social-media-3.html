The App Economy

The Gig Turn and What Came After

A narrative for Unit 10, part three

The Moment the App Store Opened

On July 10, 2008, Apple opened the App Store. Five hundred applications were available on day one. Ten million were downloaded in the first weekend.

The App Store is a controlled marketplace. Apple reviews every application before it appears. Apple takes thirty percent of every sale. Apple can remove any application at any time for any reason, and has. You cannot install software on an iPhone through any channel Apple has not approved. The device is yours; the software ecosystem is theirs.

This was presented as a security feature. It is also a business model. The iPhone created a population of captive consumers, and the App Store was the only store they were allowed to shop in. If you wanted to reach iPhone users with software, you paid Apple thirty percent. If Apple decided your application competed with one of theirs, they could remove you from the store. If Apple decided your application violated a guideline — a guideline they could change without notice — you were gone.

The Open Letter to Hobbyists had said: pay for software, or good software won't be written. The App Store said: write software, give us thirty percent, or you don't exist. The direction of obligation reversed. Now the platform extracted from the developer, not from the user.

This is the structure everything that followed was built on.

Uber and the Discovery of the Floor

Uber launched in San Francisco in 2010. The founding idea was simple: what if a car could arrive whenever you needed one, summoned by a phone?

The experience in the early years was remarkable. The app was elegant. The car arrived. The price was lower than a taxi. Sometimes dramatically lower. Uber was subsidizing rides — using venture capital to make every ride cheaper than cost, in order to grow the network, to attract more drivers, to attract more riders, to grow the network. The subsidy was enormous. It ran for years.

People adjusted their lives around it. Why own a car in the city? Why deal with parking? Uber was fast and cheap and reliable. Businesses adjusted around it. The car service industry reorganized. Taxi medallion values, which had been stable investments for decades, collapsed.

Then, when the network was large enough and the alternatives had been sufficiently disrupted, the prices started rising.

This is a known playbook. It has a name in economics: predatory pricing. Sell below cost until competitors are gone, then raise prices once the market is captured. It is illegal in some contexts. In the gig economy it was not merely tolerated — it was celebrated as innovation.

The price rise was not the only thing that changed. The driver terms changed. The take rate — the percentage Uber kept of each fare — increased. The driver pay decreased. The flexibility that had attracted drivers — work when you want, stop when you want — became a kind of precarity. Work when the algorithm needs you, stop when it doesn't, absorb the risk of the slow period and the empty stretch.

Drivers were not employees. They had no benefits, no unemployment insurance, no workers' compensation, no guaranteed minimum wage. They were independent contractors who happened to do all their work through a single platform that controlled the price, the routing, the customer relationship, and the terms of service. The independence was legal, not actual.

The Gig as a Category

Uber was the proof of concept. The category exploded.

DoorDash, Grubhub, Instacart — food and grocery delivery, same structure. The restaurant provided the food. The shopper or driver provided the labor. The platform provided the market, the routing algorithm, and took a percentage so large that many restaurants eventually discovered the platform was consuming their margins entirely. Some restaurants signed up for delivery and lost money on every delivery order while gaining visibility they were told they needed.

TaskRabbit, Fiverr, Upwork — labor marketplace platforms. You could hire someone to assemble furniture, write copy, design a logo, program a website. The labor was disaggregated into discrete tasks. The platform matched supply and demand and took a cut. The person doing the task had no relationship with the platform beyond the transaction. No history. No seniority. No negotiating power.

Airbnb — not labor exactly, but the same structure applied to space. Your apartment as a hotel room when you're not using it. The platform took the margin. The city absorbed the consequence: the removal of long-term housing stock from the rental market, the professionalization of short-term rental by investors who bought apartments specifically for the purpose, the eviction of long-term tenants to make room for tourists who generated more revenue per night.

In each case the pattern is identical: a platform provides coordination infrastructure, extracts a percentage, owns nothing, employs no one, and is valued by investors on the premise that the network, once large enough, will be essentially impossible to replace.

The valuation of Uber at its 2019 IPO was $82 billion. It had never made a profit. It has still not, reliably, made a profit. The investors were not paying for current earnings. They were paying for the size of the network — for the future moment when the alternatives would be gone and the price could be whatever the platform decided.

The Startup Culture as Epistemology

The gig economy was made possible by a culture — the startup culture of Silicon Valley, which by the 2010s had metastasized from a regional phenomenon into a global epistemology.

The premises of this epistemology were:

  1. Growth above all. A company that is growing is correct, regardless of profitability or externalities. Growth validates the model.
  2. Disruption is value. Destroying an incumbent industry is good in itself. The destruction releases value that the incumbent was suppressing.
  3. Move fast. Speed of execution matters more than correctness. Ship it. Iterate. If it breaks something, that's information.
  4. Software eats the world. Any industry can be decomposed into a software problem. The software problem, once solved, scales without limit.

These premises are not irrational in isolation. Growth matters. Some incumbent industries were genuinely inefficient. Speed of iteration is sometimes correct. Software does scale in ways that physical infrastructure does not.

But assembled into a complete worldview, they produce a specific kind of blindness. They make it difficult to ask: what are the externalities? Who is absorbing the cost of this disruption? Is the efficiency real, or is it a subsidy paid by labor that has no leverage? Is the scaling actually creating value, or is it extracting value that was previously distributed more widely?

The taxi industry was inefficient. Uber made car transportation more convenient. But the taxi driver had a medallion — a licensed right to operate, which had value, which represented a livelihood, which could be passed to a child or sold at retirement. The Uber driver has an account that can be deactivated without notice or appeal. The efficiency gains are real. The distribution of those gains is not neutral.

The startup culture did not build in the language to ask these questions. It built in the language to dismiss them: as resistance to change, as incumbents protecting their rents, as failure to understand the long game. The questions were not answered; they were category-errored out of the conversation.

Everything Is an App

In the 2010s, the question asked about any human activity was: can this be an app?

Dog walking. An app. Tutoring. An app. Medical advice. An app. Legal advice. An app. Finding a date. An app. Finding a therapist. An app. Renting a tool. An app. Having your laundry done. An app.

The app was not a metaphor for convenience. The app was a specific technical and legal structure: a platform that intermediated a transaction, took a percentage, captured the relationship, and owned the market.

The person who walked your dog was not your dog walker. They were a contractor on a platform whose dog-walking rating was their professional identity and whose continued access to the market depended on maintaining that rating. The rating was controlled by the customer, whose review was final and unappealable, and by the platform's algorithm, which distributed work based on criteria that were not disclosed.

This is the social graph monetized at the level of labor. Your reputation is a number. Your number determines your access to work. Your access to work is mediated by a platform that you cannot leave without abandoning your number. The number is yours but the platform is not.

We accepted this as the normal structure of things because we had already accepted, for decades, that the programs on our computers were not ours, that the platform we used for communication was not ours, that the terms of service could change without our consent because they said so in the terms of service. The gig economy is not a departure from the software economy. It is the software economy applied to human bodies.

LLMs Arrive

In November 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT. Within two months it had a hundred million users — the fastest adoption of any consumer technology in history.

The response from the startup culture was immediate and totalizing: every application would now include an AI feature. Every company needed an AI strategy. Every pitch deck required a slide explaining how the product would leverage large language models. The term "AI" — which had been a precise technical term for sixty years — became a marketing category, applied to anything that involved a probability distribution over text.

This is familiar. The same thing happened to "the cloud" in 2008, to "big data" in 2012, to "blockchain" in 2017. The technology is real. The capability is real. The hype is a financial instrument, not a description.

What is different about LLMs is that they trained on the written record of human knowledge. They are, in a technical sense, a compression and recombination of every text they were trained on — including the code. The model that can write Python and explain topology and answer questions about the Quake renderer learned to do these things by reading human writing: documentation, tutorials, source code, textbooks, Stack Overflow answers, academic papers.

The people who wrote those things were not compensated. The companies that trained the models own the result. The structure is the same structure.

Stack Overflow — which was the largest public repository of programming knowledge in the world, built by volunteers who answered each other's questions for free, organized by a reputation system not unlike Uber's rating — is declining. The questions that Stack Overflow would have answered are now answered by the LLM, which learned from Stack Overflow. The commons was consumed. The model does not link back.

What Is a Thing?

The question that runs through all of this is the question in the title.

Windows was a thing you bought. Or seemed to be. The disk was a thing. The license was a legal fiction dressed as a thing.

Your Facebook data is a thing. It is stored on servers. It can be quantified and analyzed and sold. But it is made of your behavior — your choices, your connections, your expressions of preference and dislike and identity. When did it stop being yours?

The Uber driver's rating is a thing. It determines income. It can be destroyed by a single bad review. It is built from labor and time and care. But the driver cannot take it to a competitor. It belongs to Uber.

The LLM's training data is a thing. It includes your words, if you've written publicly. You cannot identify your contribution. You cannot withdraw it. It is now part of a statistical model that is owned by a corporation.

At each layer, something that seemed like it belonged to someone — the software, the data, the reputation, the text — turned out to belong to the platform. The platform owned nothing it created. It owned everything it touched.

This is the ontology of the app economy. Not what things are made of, but who they belong to, and how the belonging is constructed, and who decides when the construction changes.

The Continuity

The story of personal computing is the story of social media is the story of the gig economy is the story of LLMs. They are not four stories. They are one story, told four times, each time with a new protagonist and a new vocabulary for describing the same transfer.

The thing being transferred is always the same: the value created by distributed human activity — writing code, making connections, performing labor, generating text — captured by a platform that provides the coordination infrastructure and asserts ownership of the network.

The humanist objection is always the same: the value was created by people, using their minds and their time and their creative capacity. The platform coordinated it. Coordination is valuable. But coordination is not the same as creation. The platform did not write the code, or form the friendships, or drive the car, or answer the questions. It provided the space where these things happened and taxed them.

The civic analogy holds: a government that taxes labor is providing infrastructure in return. Roads, courts, schools, protection. The gig platform takes its percentage and provides an app. The app is real. The app is not a road.

What is a thing? A thing is what you can hold, and read, and improve, and pass on. The source code of xv6 is a thing. The GPL is a thing. The Linux kernel, which forty thousand contributors have touched in thirty years, is a thing. These things were built by people who believed that knowledge is for sharing, that the written record of human thought belongs to whoever can read it, that the tool is most powerful when it is most open.

The alternative is not a thing. It is a permission. A license. A subscription. A number that goes up and down based on criteria you don't control. A model trained on your words that doesn't remember you gave them.

We have been here before. The letter was written in 1976. Stallman responded in 1983. The browser was opened in 1993. The network was social by 2008. The labor was disaggregated by 2012. The text was consumed by 2022.

The question, each time, is the same: what are you willing to call a thing? And what are you willing to call property? And who decides?

We are still deciding.
