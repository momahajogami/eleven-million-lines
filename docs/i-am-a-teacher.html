I am a teacher.

You already know what this is. You have stood in front of young people and tried to hand them something real. You know the difference between a lesson and a transmission — between covering material and watching a child's face change because something landed.

This course is designed for transmissions.

What this is

Eleven Million Lines You Should Know is a course in reading code the way you read literature: slowly, with attention to character and context, looking for the moment when the writing reveals something true.

Eleven codebases. Eleven units. Each one paired with primary sources — manifestos, mathematical papers, letters, historical documents. The code is not separate from the culture that produced it. The Unix source code and the Lions' Commentary belong together. Richard Stallman's Emacs and the GNU Manifesto belong together. Ada Lovelace's Note G and the first programs belong together.

We are teaching children to stand inside something large and old and find their bearings.

Who this is for

The course was designed first for preschool children and the families who care for them. That is not a narrowing — it is the widest possible frame. When you design for the youngest readers, you are forced to find the essential thing, the part that cannot be simplified further because it is already the truth.

Older students can work with the same materials at greater depth. The course scales up naturally. But it starts here, with the youngest, because that is the right place to start.

What you are doing when you teach this

You are a guide in a library.

The library is enormous. The books go from floor to ceiling. Your students cannot read most of them yet. That is fine. That is the point.

You are not asking them to read everything. You are asking them to be present in a place where important things were written. You are giving them the names of the people who wrote them. You are helping them see that the writing has a shape — that code is not magic, not mystery, but a human act that left a human trace.

You are saying: These shelves are yours too. You are allowed to be here.

The library metaphor

Use it. Return to it. When a child asks what does this do? and you don't know — and you won't always know — the right answer is: Let's look. That is how you behave in a library. You look. You find out.

The teacher in this course is not a computer scientist who is explaining code to non-coders. The teacher is a reader who loves reading and is willing to read something new alongside their students.

If you are that kind of teacher, you are already qualified.

A note on Track A and Track B

The course offers two tracks:

Track A — Classical. The terminal, vim or ed, compile it yourself. The tools are part of the curriculum. Reading Carmack's source code in a terminal with vim is epistemically different from reading it in a modern IDE. Track A students inhabit the tradition.

Track B — Accompanied. Any environment the student brings. More scaffolding. The Track B student still learns that a tradition exists, learns what it looks like, and understands that they are now in relation to it — even if they don't inhabit it.

Both tracks are honest. The course has a point of view: the classical experience is worth having. It says so plainly. That directness is grounding, not exclusionary. Naming the tradition is an invitation to it.

What you need to get started

Curiosity about the material. Willingness to look at things you don't fully understand and stay curious anyway. Some comfort with text and story — because that is what code is: text, and a story about who wrote it and why.

Everything else is in the course materials.

The library is open. Your students are waiting.
