I want to master computer programming at the deepest level.

Then you are in the right place, and this is going to take a while, and that is exactly as it should be.

Maybe you started here when you were very small. Maybe an older sibling or a friend or a teacher brought you to this library when you were in preschool and something caught your eye — a name, a line of code, a story about a person who stayed up late and built something that changed the world. Maybe you are coming to this for the first time as an adult, and you want to go back to the source. Maybe you are a teenager who has already written some code and suspects there is something deeper underneath it, something older, something that would make the rest of it make sense.

All of those are the right reason to be here. Come in.

Make a plan

Here is an honest one:

Give yourself a year just to look around.

Not a year to master everything. Not a year to finish all eleven units and understand every line of every codebase. A year to survey the terrain — to walk through the library slowly, open some books, read some pages, put them down, pick up something else. To learn the names of things. To let the shape of the tradition start to become familiar.

That year is not wasted time. It is the foundation that everything else stands on. The people who try to master programming by going deep immediately into one narrow thing often find, years later, that they are missing the ground. They know one corridor of the library very well and have never seen the other rooms.

This course gives you the rooms first.

After that year — after you have walked through Early Unix and the first C compilers and the GNU philosophy and Blender and TeX and Haskell and Assembly and Ada Lovelace's notes and the mathematics of Grothendieck and Carmack's fast inverse square root and the paper that invented the transformer architecture — after all of that, you will have a map.

And then you can go anywhere on the map as far as you want for the rest of your life.

The two tracks

The course has two tracks. Both are honest about what they offer and what they cost.

Track A — Classical. The terminal. Vim. Compile it yourself. Navigate with the keyboard. Read source code in the same environment where it was written. This track is harder to enter and more rewarding to inhabit. It is not harder because the tools are better — it is harder because the tools are the tradition, and learning them means learning to stand inside something rather than looking at it through glass.

The recommendation, if you want to go deep: take Track A. Not because Track B is wrong, but because the discomfort of Track A is part of the education. Every hour you spend learning to move in a terminal is an hour you are thinking in the same register as the people who built the things you are studying.

Track B — Accompanied. Any environment you bring. More scaffolding. You still read the same codebases, encounter the same characters, receive the same history. You are in relation to the tradition even if you are not inside it. This is a real and legitimate way to learn. For some people, at some moments, it is the right choice.

The course does not pretend both tracks are identical. It names the difference clearly and lets you choose.

What mastery actually looks like

It does not look like finishing.

The codebases in this course — the eleven of them — are not puzzles to solve. They are more like parks, or cathedrals, or old forests. You can walk through them in an afternoon. You can also spend years returning to them and finding new things. Ken Thompson's process scheduler in xv6 is twenty pages long and you can read it in an hour. You can also spend months with it, tracing every line, understanding every decision, reading the papers that informed it, reading the responses to those papers, learning the history of every fork in the road where Thompson chose one thing instead of another.

That depth is always available. The surface visit and the lifetime of study are both real relationships with the same text.

Mastery, in this sense, is not a destination. It is a practice. It is the ongoing willingness to go back to something you have already read and read it again with more experience, and find that it has more to say.

This is your heritage

The eleven codebases in this course are part of the world you were born into. You did not have to earn access to them. They are not behind a paywall or a credential or a prerequisites list.

They are open source. They are on the internet. They were made by people who believed, in most cases very strongly, that they should be shared — that code, like writing, belongs to whoever is willing to read it.

That belief is itself part of the curriculum. Richard Stallman wrote the GNU Manifesto because he thought software freedom was a moral issue. Linus Torvalds released his kernel under a license that guaranteed it would stay open. Donald Knuth made TeX free for anyone to use forever. The codebases in this course are not just technically available — they were made available on purpose, by people who wanted you to have them.

You are welcome here. Your presence in this library is something the people who built it hoped for.

Study for more than yourself

You can practice with a larger intention.

The tradition you are entering is full of people who thought their work mattered beyond themselves — who believed that building a good operating system or writing a good compiler or proving a theorem about topology was not just a personal achievement but a contribution to something common, something that would outlast them and be used by people they would never meet.

That intention is available to you too.

When you study carefully, you are keeping something alive. When you understand something deeply and then explain it clearly, you are extending the reach of the tradition. When you build something and share it, you are adding to a library that other people will walk into someday, maybe when they are very small, and find something that catches their eye.

The effort to learn and improve is not a private act. It ripples outward in ways you cannot fully predict or see. The person who studies hard and focuses and tries to understand at the deepest level — they become someone who can help. Not just themselves. Everyone around them. And through the things they build and write and teach, people they will never know.

Study for yourself. And let your studying be for everyone.

Where to begin

Start at Unit 01. Read the orientation. Open the source code. Don't try to understand everything. Look at the shape of it. Read the names of the functions. Follow one thread until it leads somewhere interesting, then stop, write down what you noticed, and go back to the beginning.

Do that for a year.

Everything else follows.

The library is open. The books go from floor to ceiling. You have your whole life.
