# The Right to Read

*A philosophical essay for Unit 10*

---

## The Law Analogy

Consider a simple rule: you are not allowed to read the law.

Not that the law is complicated, or technical, or written in language that requires training to parse — those things are true and important. Something simpler and more absolute: the text of the law is proprietary. You may be governed by it. You may be punished for violating it. But you may not read it without a license, and the license is held by the government, and the government may revoke it.

This would be recognized immediately, across nearly every tradition of political thought, as a form of tyranny. The publication of law — the insistence that the rules governing people's lives be available for those people to read — is not a courtesy. It is a precondition of legitimate governance. We have a word for law that operates in secret: arbitrary power. The Magna Carta was, among other things, a demand that the king's rules be written down and made available. The printing of statutes in the vernacular — not Latin, not the language of clergy and scholars, but the language people actually spoke — was a political act. The publication of legal decisions, so that citizens could know not just what the law said but how it had been applied, is what makes a legal system something other than the private preferences of whoever holds power today.

"Ignorance of the law is no excuse" — the legal maxim that sounds like a threat — is only tolerable because the law is available to be known. Remove the availability and the maxim becomes a trap: punished for violating rules you were not permitted to read.

We understand all of this. We share it, across political differences that go very deep. Even people who agree on almost nothing about governance agree that secret law is illegitimate. It is one of the few points where liberal and conservative, anarchist and authoritarian, find the same answer.

Then consider code.

## The Code That Governs You

The software running on your phone right now governs your behavior in ways that are at least as consequential as most of the laws in your jurisdiction.

The recommendation algorithm decides what you see. Not just what is available — what appears, in what order, with what prominence, weighted by what criteria. The spam filter decides which communications reach you. The credit scoring algorithm decides whether you can borrow money, at what rate, for what purpose. The content moderation system decides whether your speech is permitted. The terms of service — written by lawyers to be as broad as legally sustainable — specify the conditions under which all of this can change without your consent.

These are not suggestions. They are the rules of the systems in which your life happens. They govern communication, finance, information access, social connection. They are, in any meaningful sense, law — applied to your life, with consequences for violation, administered by entities with power over you.

And you are not allowed to read them.

Not in the sense that they are technically inaccessible — some of this code is open source, some has been partially disclosed through legal proceedings or leaks, some has been reverse-engineered by researchers. In the sense that the systems that designed these platforms have consistently asserted the right to keep the governing logic secret, and this assertion has been largely upheld legally and almost entirely accepted culturally.

We accepted it. That is the remarkable thing. We accepted it not because someone forced us to, but because we were gradually and incrementally committed to a framework — built up over fifty years, from the Open Letter to Hobbyists through the EULA to the Terms of Service — in which the code was property, and reading property you don't own is trespass.

---

## How the Commitment Was Made

It did not happen all at once. It never does.

In 1976 it was a reasonable-sounding claim: Bill Gates had written software and people were copying it without paying. The claim was economic. The remedy seemed proportionate. You should pay for the work that went into creating a thing.

But embedded in that claim was a prior commitment: that the thing was property, that code was the kind of thing that could be owned in the way a chair is owned, that reading it without permission was the same kind of act as taking something that wasn't yours. This commitment was not argued for. It was assumed, and then the assumption accumulated legal weight.

By the time personal computers were ubiquitous — by the time you could not live a normal professional or social life without running software — the framework was already fixed. The code that ran your word processor was not yours to read. The code that ran your operating system was not yours to read. The code that ran your browser, your email client, your financial software: none of it yours to read, and the cultural expectation was that you would not try.

This happened at exactly the moment when it mattered most. The computer arrived as the most powerful tool for reading and thinking that had ever been built. A machine that could externalize reasoning, organize information, make visible the structure of complex systems. A machine that, if you could read the code that ran it, would teach you things about logic and process and structure that no previous tool had made available to ordinary people.

And the code was closed, just then, just as the possibility was becoming real.

Stallman recognized this as a kind of violence — not physical, but epistemic. The tool arrived. The tool was made unreadable. The potential was locked inside the tool. You could use it but not understand it. You could operate it but not know it. You became, in relation to the most important technology of the century, a user. Not a reader. Not a maker. A consumer of operations you could not examine.

## Stallman's Story

In 1997, Richard Stallman wrote a short piece of dystopian fiction called "The Right to Read." It imagined a near future in which all reading material — books, articles, academic papers — is licensed, and sharing unlicensed reading material is a criminal offense. The protagonist, a student named Dan, has working software on his computer. His classmate Lissa's computer has broken down the night before an important exam. He could let her use his laptop. But if she reads his software, he has shared it without a license, and the consequences could end his career.

He gives her the laptop anyway. She passes the exam. He later finds out that the university's software monitors what is read and reports unlicensed access. He waits for consequences that may never come, or may come without warning.

Stallman labeled it a story about the future. It is also a description of the present. The monitoring is real. The licensing terms that prohibit sharing are real. The criminal consequences for circumventing copy protection — established by the Digital Millennium Copyright Act of 1998 — are real. Researchers who have attempted to study the algorithms that govern social media have faced legal threats under terms of service that prohibit reverse engineering.

The story was satire when he wrote it. It has become reportage.

## What It Did to Us

The right to read is not only a political right. It is a cognitive one.

A person who can read the systems that govern them is in a different epistemic position than one who cannot. Not just more informed — more capable of a particular kind of agency. The capacity to examine the mechanism, to understand how an outcome was produced, to trace a decision back to the logic that generated it: this is what it means to be a participant in a system rather than a subject of it.

We built, collectively, the most powerful tools for extending human cognitive capacity that have ever existed. And we deployed them in a configuration that made them, for most people, cognitively opaque. Black boxes that receive input and produce output, and the transformation between input and output is not your business.

This is the brain snipping. Not a metaphor — a structural fact. The tools that could have extended our capacity to think about complex systems were deployed in a way that required us not to think about the complex systems. The expertise was concentrated. The literacy was rationed. The reading was forbidden.

The consequence is not just political, though it is political. It is also a consequence for what we are. For what kind of beings we became in relation to the technology that now shapes so much of our experience. We are beings who operate systems we cannot examine, which means we are beings who cannot fully understand the conditions of our own existence.

This is a strong claim. It is meant to be.

## The Continuity With Law

The analogy to law is not decorative. It is precise.

Law is language that governs behavior. Its legitimacy depends on its availability to be read by those it governs. The publication requirement is not a courtesy — it is what distinguishes law from arbitrary power.

Code is language that governs behavior. Its legitimacy — as a system for organizing human activity, for coordinating action, for mediating conflict — ought to depend on the same availability. The algorithm that decides your credit score is making a consequential judgment about your life. The terms under which that judgment is made should be available to you, the same way the statute under which you might be taxed or arrested is available to you.

We do not apply this standard to code. We apply almost no standards at all. The company that built the algorithm owes you no explanation. The model that made the decision cannot be examined. The terms of service that govern your relationship to the platform that governs your speech are written by the platform's lawyers to maximize the platform's options and minimize your recourse.

This is secret law. We have a word for that.

The word is not "software licensing." The word is not "intellectual property protection." The word is tyranny, used carefully and without hyperbole. A system that governs people's lives through rules they are not permitted to read and cannot challenge is a tyranny of whatever size it operates at. That a corporation exercises this kind of power rather than a government does not change the structure. It changes the remedies.

## The Course as a Response

This is why reading code is a civic act.

Not in the vague sense that technical literacy is good citizenship. In the precise sense that the right to read the systems that govern your life is a precondition of meaningful participation in those systems, and the exercise of that right — the actual reading, the actual examination — is an assertion of that precondition.

When you read xv6, you are reading the structure of an operating system — the kind of structure that, in a closed version, is the thing you are not permitted to read. When you read the Quake source, you are reading the decisions that went into building a real-time rendering system — decisions that, in a modern game engine, are trade secrets. When you read the TCP/IP stack, you are reading the protocol that every communication on the internet uses — which is public, which is why the internet works, which is an existence proof that openness is a viable design.

The tradition this course belongs to is the tradition of publication — the insistence that the written record of human decisions about how systems should work belongs to the people who live under those systems. This is not a radical position. It is the position that produced libraries, and public education, and the printing of statutes in the vernacular, and the open internet.

It is the position that Stallman articulated in 1983, that the GPL encodes in legal language, that every open source project enacts by existing. It is the position that the proprietary software model contradicts, and that the social media platform contradicts, and that the algorithmic system contradicts.

The course does not pretend the contradiction has been resolved. It has not been resolved. It is the live argument of the present.

What the course does is take a side. The side is: the code should be readable. The law should be public. The systems that govern your life should be available for your examination. Not because this is easy, or commercially convenient, or the default that emerged from the market. Because anything less is a diminishment of what you are.

## Coda: The Preschooler

A three-year-old cannot read code. A three-year-old cannot read law. A three-year-old is learning to read — which is to say, learning that the world contains systems of meaning that can be entered, that the marks on the page are not arbitrary, that the sounds and symbols correspond to something real, that reading is a form of power.

The three-year-old who grows up knowing that code is readable — that it is a form of writing, that writing is continuous with speaking and thinking and acting, that the systems that govern their life are made of language they can learn to read — is in a different position than the one who grows up treating the computer as an appliance. Not because they will necessarily become a programmer. Because they will know that the mechanism is accessible, that the decision can be examined, that the rule is not magic.

That knowledge changes what you are. Not all at once. Over a lifetime of encounters with systems that want you to stay outside.

*Stay outside* is not the position of this course.
