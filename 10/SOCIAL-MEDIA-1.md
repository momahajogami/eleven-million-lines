# The Desire to Be Seen
## Social Media Before Facebook

*A narrative for Unit 10*

---

## The Feeling First

Before the technology, there was the feeling.

You were online. You had a connection — dial-up, maybe broadband by the early 2000s. You could reach servers anywhere in the world. You could read news, download software, look up anything. The internet was enormous and getting larger every day.

And you could not see your friends.

This sounds small. It wasn't. The people you knew — your actual people, the ones you ate lunch with and called on the phone and thought about — were not there. The internet was full of strangers and information and commerce, and the people who mattered to you were absent from it entirely. You couldn't see what they were doing. You couldn't see what they looked like, in the way a photo shows you. You couldn't make a profile. You couldn't say: *this is me, here I am, this is what I care about.*

The desire to be seen — to have a presence, to exist online in a way that connected to your actual life — was real and had nowhere to go. It built pressure through the 1990s. The technologies that eventually released that pressure were not built to release it. They stumbled into it.

---

## Email and the First Proof

**Email** was the first thing that worked.

Not web-based email — not yet. First, email on university servers, then on ISP accounts (CompuServe, AOL, eventually Hotmail). You had an address. The address was yours. If you gave it to someone and they gave you theirs, you could write to them directly, across any distance, for free.

This is not nothing. Before email, staying in touch with people required effort proportional to distance. A letter took days and cost a stamp. A long-distance phone call cost real money, charged by the minute. People lost touch because staying in touch was expensive. Email made it free and nearly instant.

The effect on friendship was quiet but cumulative. You could write to a college friend who had moved away. You could keep a conversation going with someone you'd met briefly and wanted to know better. The social graph — the actual network of your relationships — didn't have to contract when geography changed. It could stay open.

But email was private. It was one-to-one, or one-to-mailing-list. It was not a presence. Receiving an email from someone told you they thought of you in that moment; it did not tell you who they were becoming, what they were doing, what their life looked like day to day. You still couldn't see them.

## Early Radio

No one knew what the internet was for.

This is important to say plainly, because looking back it is easy to see the arc — BBSs to Usenet to the web to social media — as inevitable, as if the destination was always visible. It was not. The people building in the 1990s and early 2000s were genuinely experimental. They were trying things. Most of the things didn't work.

The analogy that fits is **early radio**. When broadcasting began in the 1920s, nobody knew what radio was for. The first stations played music, read poetry, broadcast sermons, transmitted weather reports, let people talk about whatever they wanted. A farmer in Iowa could speak and be heard in Chicago. This was extraordinary and nobody knew what to do with it. The first commercial radio station, KDKA in Pittsburgh, broadcast the 1920 presidential election results as its inaugural program, not because election results were the obvious use of radio, but because someone had to broadcast something and that was what was happening.

The form took a decade to find itself: the variety show, the soap opera, the news broadcast, the comedy hour. These formats were not obvious in advance. They emerged from experiment, from finding out what people wanted to hear, from the interaction between the medium and the audience.

The early social web was in the same phase. Nobody knew what it was for. You could try anything.

## Forums and the Proto-Self

Before profiles, there were **forums**.

Forums were threaded discussion boards: topics, replies, nested conversations. phpBB, vBulletin, Simple Machines. Every subculture had forums. Skateboarding forums, music forums, forums for every band and sports team and TV show. Forums for people who had specific medical conditions, forums for people in specific cities, forums for people who liked specific kinds of coffee.

The forum gave you a username and an avatar — a small image, usually 100 by 100 pixels — and a post count and a signature. These were crude instruments for self-expression, but they were instruments. Your username was a name you chose. Your avatar was something you selected to represent you. Your signature could contain a quote you loved or a link to something you made. Your post count was a measure of your investment in the community.

People cared about these things. They agonized over usernames. They changed avatars seasonally. A high post count was social capital. You were somebody in this forum if you had been here long enough and said enough true things.

But the forum was vertical. You were somebody *here*, in this specific community, on this specific topic. You weren't yourself — you weren't the full, cross-cutting, complicated actual person — you were your forum self, which was real but partial. There was no place online where all of you was present.

## Friendster and the Proof of Concept

**Friendster** launched in 2002. Jonathan Abrams built it in his apartment in San Francisco. The idea was simple: make your social network visible. You created a profile — a real profile, with your name and your photo and some things about yourself — and you connected it to profiles of people you actually knew. Their profiles linked to profiles of people *they* knew. You could navigate the network. You could see who knew whom. You could see that your friend from college knew your coworker, that you were three steps from someone you wanted to meet.

The name was the argument: a portmanteau of "friend" and "Napster." Napster had used peer-to-peer networking to share music; Friendster would use social networking to share people.

It worked. Within a few months of launch, Friendster had a million users. Within a year, it had several million. Google offered to acquire it for $30 million in 2003. Abrams declined, believing Friendster was worth more.

Then the servers fell over.

Friendster's infrastructure could not handle the load. The database queries for social graph traversal — "show me everything about this person, and everyone connected to them, and rate of interaction, and..." — were slow and getting slower as the network grew. Page loads took thirty seconds. Then sixty. Users waited and grew frustrated and left.

This is the Friendster lesson: a social network is a distributed systems problem. The engineering is not a feature — it is the foundation. Without the engineering, the idea doesn't matter.

Friendster never recovered from the performance problems. It survived in Southeast Asia, where it remained popular until 2011, and then shut down. Abrams's decision not to sell to Google is often cited as one of the great missed opportunities in Silicon Valley history.

## MySpace and the Bedroom Wall

**MySpace** launched in August 2003, built in ten days by a team at eUniverse (later Intermix Media) after watching Friendster's success. The founders — Tom Anderson, Chris DeWolfe, and others — were not building something philosophically distinct from Friendster. They were building Friendster faster, for the people Friendster was failing.

What made MySpace different was almost an accident: **you could edit your profile with HTML and CSS**.

This was not a feature so much as a failure to prevent it. Users discovered they could put `<div>` tags and `<style>` blocks in their profile text fields and the page would render them. The developers noticed. They did not stop it.

The result was one of the strangest and most vibrant aesthetic periods in the history of the web. MySpace profiles became personal expression in the most baroque sense: tiled backgrounds of stars or flames or band logos, multiple animated GIFs, text in seventeen colors, embedded music players that started automatically when you loaded the page, layouts that required horizontal scrolling, mood indicators, visitor counters. It was ugly, often inaccessible, technically a disaster — and completely, personally, specifically *yours*.

This is what a bedroom wall looks like. Before MySpace, your bedroom wall might have band posters and photos and ticket stubs and drawings and magazine clippings. It was chaotic and personal and entirely the point. MySpace was the first online space that let you do the equivalent. The ugliness was the authenticity.

Tom Anderson — "Tom from MySpace," whose grinning profile photo became the default friend of every new user — understood the music angle early. MySpace let unsigned bands post their music. A generation of musicians built audiences on MySpace before any label showed interest. Arctic Monkeys, Lily Allen, Colbie Caillat: the MySpace origin story became a genre. The platform was, briefly, a genuine music discovery engine.

News Corp acquired MySpace in July 2005 for $580 million. Rupert Murdoch believed the internet was the future and MySpace was his way in. The acquisition killed what made MySpace MySpace: the scrappy, user-first, anything-goes culture was replaced by corporate caution and advertising pressure. The profile editing was slowly restricted. The performance, always poor, got worse. When Facebook opened to everyone in September 2006, MySpace was already dying.

## LiveJournal and Tumblr and the Space Between

Not everyone wanted a social network. Some people wanted a diary.

**LiveJournal** (1999) was a blogging platform that added social features: you followed people, they followed you, you could comment on each other's entries. It was popular with teenagers and young adults and writers and people with specific obsessive interests. It had a commenting culture that was genuinely literary — long, thoughtful, personal. Fanfiction communities formed on LiveJournal that produced work as carefully crafted as anything published commercially.

LiveJournal was also the first place many people came out online. The pseudonymous diary format — you could be anonymous, or semi-anonymous, or gradually more yourself — was a space for trying out identities before you had to commit to them in life. For a generation of queer teenagers in places where being queer was dangerous, LiveJournal was sometimes the only place that was safe.

It was acquired by a Russian company, SUP Media, in 2007. The culture shifted. Many users left for other platforms.

**Tumblr** (2007) is harder to categorize. It was a microblogging platform — shorter than LiveJournal, more visual, with a reblogging system that let content propagate through networks of followers the way a meme propagates. But Tumblr was also an art platform, a fanfiction archive, a social space for young queer people, a home for niche aesthetics and subcultures that had nowhere else to go.

The reblog was Tumblr's key invention. You saw something — a photo, a gif, a poem, a piece of art, a political argument — and you pressed a button and it appeared on your blog with attribution back to the original. The network was not a friend graph; it was a taste graph. You followed people whose aesthetic you admired, not necessarily people you knew. Content moved through the network by resonance.

Tumblr had no advertising mechanism that worked. It was acquired by Yahoo in 2013 for $1.1 billion. Yahoo did not know what to do with it. Tumblr banned adult content in 2018 in a catastrophic moderation decision that eliminated a large portion of its user base overnight. In 2019, Yahoo (then Verizon) sold Tumblr to Automattic (the company behind WordPress) for an amount rumored to be under $3 million — a loss of more than $1 billion in six years.

The Tumblr arc is the story of a platform that served a real community — that *was* a real community — and was acquired by companies that didn't understand what they had bought and couldn't build a business around it. The community dispersed. Some went to Twitter. Some built their own spaces. Some are still looking for where Tumblr went.

## What the Experimental Era Proved

By 2007, the social web had established several things:

**Profiles work.** People want to exist online as themselves, with photos and names and self-descriptions. They will put real effort into a profile if the platform earns their trust.

**Networks are the product.** The value is not in any single person's profile; it is in the connection between profiles. Friendster understood this and built for it; its infrastructure couldn't hold it.

**Culture is load-bearing.** MySpace's culture was what made MySpace. News Corp bought the users but not the culture, and the culture was what the users were there for.

**The demographic matters.** Each platform attracted a distinct community — not just by age or geography, but by sensibility. LiveJournal was writers. Tumblr was artists and teenagers and queer people and fandom. MySpace was music. No platform served everyone; the platforms that tried to serve everyone lost the specificity that made them worth serving.

**Nobody knew what it was for.** The best thing about this era was that nobody was optimizing yet. The advertising infrastructure was primitive. The engagement metrics were unsophisticated. There was no algorithmic feed deciding what you saw. You chose who to follow and you saw what they posted. The experience was chaotic and often wonderful.

This is the early radio phase. The form is finding itself. What it will become is not yet determined. The people building are experimenting, not executing against a plan.

## Then It Was 2008

In September 2006, Facebook opened to anyone with an email address. Until then, it had been restricted to college students — first Harvard, then the Ivy League, then universities broadly. The walled-garden strategy was deliberate: the scarcity created trust, and the trust made the network real.

When it opened, it grew. Then it grew faster. Then it grew in a way that social platforms had not grown before.

The News Feed had launched a week earlier, in September 2006 — a stream of updates from everyone you were connected to, continuously refreshed, algorithmically sorted. Users hated it immediately and loudly. Hundreds of thousands signed petitions against it. Facebook did not roll it back. Within weeks, usage of the News Feed was so high that the complaints stopped. People had hated it because it was new and because it felt like surveillance. They kept using it because it was addictive in the precise sense: it delivered variable rewards at variable intervals, the same mechanism as a slot machine.

By 2008, something had changed that had not changed with any previous social platform. Facebook was not a place you went. It was a thing you had. It was infrastructure.

Your relationship status was on Facebook. Your birthday reminders were on Facebook. The photos from the party last weekend were on Facebook. If you weren't on Facebook, you missed the conversation; if you were on Facebook, you had already had it before you talked in person. The platform had become the substrate for social life in a way that MySpace and Friendster had pointed toward but never achieved.

The experimental era was over. Something real had arrived.
