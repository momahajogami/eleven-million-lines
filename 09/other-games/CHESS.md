# Chess and the Machine

*A game of pure reason. The question it asked, and what happened when we answered it.*

---

## The Mechanical Turk, 1770

In 1770, Wolfgang von Kempelen presented to the court of Empress Maria Theresa of Austria an automaton that could play chess. The figure wore Turkish robes. It sat before a large wooden cabinet. It moved its arm, picked up pieces, set them back down. It played. It won.

Over the following eighty years the Turk toured Europe and America, defeating Napoleon Bonaparte — who tried to cheat and was corrected — and Benjamin Franklin, who lost graciously. It was examined by skeptics, inspected by the curious, studied by engineers. It remained apparently inexplicable.

The Turk was a fraud. The cabinet concealed a chess master who manipulated the automaton's arm through a system of levers. The interior was rearranged before each public inspection so that no single angle revealed the person inside. The fraud was elaborate enough to hold for decades.

But the Turk asked a question that was not fraudulent: *can a machine play chess?*

Not a machine concealing a person. A machine.

Charles Babbage encountered the Turk and was fascinated. Edgar Allan Poe wrote an essay arguing it must be a fraud — machines, he insisted, could not think. The question haunted the early history of computing because chess was understood as a game of pure reason: no luck, no hidden information, no physical skill. If a machine could play chess well, the implications for what machines could do were vast.

The question would take two hundred years to answer. The answer changed everything about who has access to the game.

---

## The expensive dream

For most of the twentieth century, playing against a strong chess engine required money.

The first serious chess programs ran on mainframes in the 1950s and 1960s — machines owned by universities and governments. Alan Turing sketched a chess-playing algorithm on paper in 1951 and played it by hand, simulating the machine's moves himself, because no computer available to him was fast enough to run it. Claude Shannon wrote the theoretical framework for computer chess in 1950. These were thought experiments made executable only on hardware that few people could touch.

By the 1980s, home computers could run weak chess programs: Chessmaster, Sargon, CHESS. You could buy them on floppy disks for your Apple II or Commodore 64. They were beatable by any serious amateur. The commercial chess engine market grew: Fritz, Junior, Hiarcs, Shredder — each stronger than the last, each sold as boxed software for $40, $60, $100.

If you wanted to study with a strong engine, you bought software. If you could not afford it, you did not have access. The engine was a luxury good, like a private teacher or a well-stocked library.

This was the world as it stood on May 11, 1997.

---

## Deep Blue

On that day, IBM's Deep Blue defeated Garry Kasparov in the sixth game of a six-game match, winning the series 3½–2½. Kasparov was the world chess champion — the strongest human player alive. He had defeated a previous version of Deep Blue in 1996. He did not expect to lose.

Deep Blue was not a home computer. It was a purpose-built parallel system: 30 nodes, each containing a general-purpose processor and 16 specialized chess chips. It evaluated 200 million positions per second. It cost tens of millions of dollars to build. After the match, IBM retired it. There was no commercial version. You could not buy Deep Blue.

Kasparov's loss was registered as something historic and slightly ominous. A machine had surpassed the best human at the most cerebral game humans played. The question that Babbage and Poe had asked in the shadow of the Mechanical Turk was definitively answered.

And the answer raised a new question: if the machine is better, what does that mean for the human?

For the people who played chess in clubs on Saturday afternoons, who read annotated grandmaster games in paperback books, who played blitz games in parks — the immediate answer was: not much, practically. Deep Blue existed in another world. It was corporate property, retired after the match, inaccessible. The philosophical unease was real. The practical change was not yet here.

But a change was coming, and it would not come from IBM.

---

## The ethical charge

The Mechanical Turk poses a question that sharpens with every passing year: what does it mean to live in a world where every phone can beat a grandmaster?

It is easy to frame this as loss. The skill of the grandmaster, accumulated over decades, can be surpassed by a $200 device with no understanding of what it is doing. If a machine can defeat Magnus Carlsen, what is the point of becoming Magnus Carlsen?

But this is the wrong frame.

Before Stockfish, before free engines, chess mastery was in part a function of access. The players who could afford strong engines for analysis, strong databases for preparation, strong coaching — they improved faster. The economics of chess reinforced existing advantages. Wealth bought access to strength.

What free engines changed was not the *value* of chess understanding but the *distribution* of access to it. The person who could not afford Fritz or Hiarcs or a grandmaster coach now has Stockfish, which is stronger than any of them. The child in rural Ohio and the child in rural Nigeria and the child in a Moscow apartment block all have the same access to the strongest analysis tool ever built.

The game did not become easier. It became more equally hard.

And the magic of chess — the thing that the Mechanical Turk audiences already sensed, before they knew the Turk was a fraud — did not evaporate when machines got strong. The game kept being played. The game kept being beautiful. The wooden pieces kept awakening something in people that coins and playing cards do not.

The machine answers the chess question. The chess question remains interesting.
